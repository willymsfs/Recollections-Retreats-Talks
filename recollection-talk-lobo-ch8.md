## OPENING: THE PROBLEMATIC OF STATE EXECUTION
*(7 minutes)*

By way of introducing this eighth reflection in our series, let me begin with a theological affirmation of the most fundamental kind — and then with a reality that contradicts it in ways the Church has taken far too long to name with the clarity the Gospel demands.

We say: every human being is made in the image of God — the *imago Dei* of *Genesis 1:27*. This is the foundational anthropological claim of the entire Judaeo-Christian tradition. It is not qualified by behavior, by criminal record, by the severity of the offense committed, or by the judgment of any human tribunal. The *imago Dei* is ontological — it belongs to the structure of the human person as created and loved by God, prior to and independent of everything the person does or fails to do. And Jesus, in *John 10:10*, states his entire mission in terms of this anthropological conviction: *"I came that they may have life, and have it in fullness."* Not some of them. Not the ones who deserve it. All of them. In fullness.

That is the claim.

And now the reality.

On the morning of March 20, 2020 — three days before India entered its first COVID lockdown — four men were executed by hanging in Tihar Jail. The Nirbhaya case. A crime of exceptional brutality, a judicial process of seven years, and a sentence carried out as the country held its breath. The public response, across social media, in television studios, in ordinary living rooms, was celebration. People who would otherwise describe themselves as compassionate, as reform-minded, as followers of a Gospel of mercy — celebrated.

I am not here to defend the crime. What was done to that young woman was monstrous. No serious person argues otherwise. I am here to ask the question that the celebration obscured: does the monstrousness of a crime confer upon the state the authority to take the life of the person who committed it? And I am here to note that the Church — which follows a man who was himself executed by the state, legally, officially, with the full procedure of Roman judicial authority — has now answered that question with formal doctrinal clarity.

Pope Francis, incorporating into the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* the development of doctrine articulated in paragraph 2267 (revised 2018), said: *"The death penalty is inadmissible because it is an attack on the inviolability and dignity of the person. The Church works with determination for its abolition worldwide."*

Not *"the Church has reservations."* Not *"the Church encourages pastoral discernment."* **Inadmissible.** The Church works *with determination* for its abolition **worldwide**. That is a doctrinal position, formally incorporated into the Catechism. And it has been, in the formation houses and pulpits of India, almost entirely unpreached.

The gap between that doctrinal clarity and its absence from our pastoral practice is the problematic of this reflection.

In one sentence, if I were to state the thesis: **The Church's opposition to capital punishment is not a political position contingent on social context but a theological conviction flowing directly from the *imago Dei*, from the Christological claim of John 10:10, and from the ecclesiological self-understanding of a community that follows a God who was executed by the state and raised to vindicate every human being the world has discarded — and the consecrated person's silence on this matter is not neutrality but a form of complicity.**

Let me develop this across four movements.

## PART I — TWO THEOLOGIES OF PUNISHMENT: RETRIBUTIVE AND RESTORATIVE
*(10 minutes)*

There are two operative theologies of punishment that have coexisted, in tension, throughout the history of Christian civilization — and indeed throughout the history of human legal thought. Their conflict is not merely juridical. It is theological. And the position one takes on capital punishment is, ultimately, determined by which theology of punishment one inhabits.

I shall call them the *retributive theology* and the *restorative theology* of punishment.

**The retributive theology** understands the purpose of punishment as the proportionate return of harm for harm. In this model, justice is fundamentally a matter of equivalence: the criminal has taken something of value — life, property, security, dignity — and justice requires that something of equivalent value be taken from the criminal in return. The scales of justice are the symbol of this theology: balance, equivalence, reciprocity. The ultimate logic of retributive theology, when applied consistently, is capital punishment: you have taken a life; therefore your life is taken. The equation is resolved. Justice is served.

The retributive theology has deep roots in human legal tradition. It has received theological articulation from serious thinkers. And it responds to a genuine moral intuition: that wrongdoing has consequences, that there is a proper proportion between offense and response, and that the failure to hold offenders accountable is itself a form of injustice.

But the retributive theology, I want to argue, contains a structural theological error that the Christian tradition must name. The error is this: it locates the purpose of punishment entirely in the past — in the offense committed — and has no theological account of the future of the person being punished. The offender, in the retributive model, is defined entirely by what they have done. Their identity is the crime. And if the crime is sufficient, the identity is forfeit. The retributive theology, taken to its limit, is a theology of despair about the human person: it holds that some people are, finally and irreversibly, beyond the reach of the God who creates, redeems, and transforms.

This is the point Sr Inigo names with surgical precision: *condemning someone to death is an act of despair.* It is society saying — formally, legally, irrevocably — that this person has no future, that transformation is impossible, that the God who makes all things new has reached the limit of His capacity in this particular case.

**The restorative theology** of punishment understands its purpose entirely differently. Punishment, in this model, exists not to balance scales but to restore persons — to the community, to themselves, and to the God in whose image they were made. This theology does not minimize the offense. It is not naive about the severity of what has been done or the genuine need to protect the community from further harm. But it holds that the purpose of any response to wrongdoing must be oriented toward the future: toward the transformation of the offender, the healing of the victim, and the restoration of the social fabric that the crime has torn.

This is the theology consistent with the entire prophetic and Wisdom tradition of the Hebrew Scriptures. *Ezekiel 18:23* — *"Have I any pleasure in the death of the wicked, says the Lord God, and not rather that they should turn from their ways and live?"* The question is rhetorical. God has no pleasure in the death of the wicked. God's purpose is always turning — *metanoia*, the complete reorientation of a life — and life. The restorative theology takes this divine orientation seriously and builds its account of punishment upon it.

And it is the theology consistent with the New Testament, where the model of response to wrongdoing is never the balance sheet but always the embrace. The father who ran toward the returning son before the son had finished his rehearsed speech of remorse. The Jesus who said to the woman caught in adultery — whose guilt was not in question, whose execution was legally mandated — *"Neither do I condemn you. Go your way, and from now on do not sin again"* (*John 8:11*). The Jesus who said, from the cross, to a convicted criminal who had no time left for rehabilitation: *"Today you will be with me in paradise"* (*Luke 23:43*).

The restorative theology is not soft on crime. It is hard on despair. It refuses the conclusion that any human person is finally beyond the reach of the God who breathed life into them in the first place.

## PART II — ETYMOLOGICAL RECOVERY: WHAT *IMAGO DEI* ACTUALLY CLAIMS
*(8 minutes)*

Let me now perform the etymological recovery that this argument requires — returning a foundational theological phrase to its original and most demanding force.

The Latin *imago Dei* — *image of God* — translates the Hebrew *tselem Elohim* of *Genesis 1:27*: *"God created humankind in his image; in the image of God he created them; male and female he created them."* The Hebrew *tselem* means, literally, a sculptured figure, a representation — the kind of image that a king would erect in a conquered territory to assert his sovereignty and presence. In the ancient Near Eastern context, the *tselem* of the king was placed in territories the king could not physically occupy, as a sign that his authority extended there nonetheless.

When Genesis uses this term for the human person, the claim is breathtaking in its scope: every human being is a living *tselem* of God — a physical, embodied, particular, unrepeatable representation of the divine presence in the world. Not a symbol. Not a metaphor. A *tselem* — a sculptured figure bearing the king's own form, asserting the king's presence wherever it stands.

Now, what has happened to *imago Dei* in the dominant discourse of criminal justice — and, more disturbingly, in the implicit theological anthropology of communities that support capital punishment — is, once again, a conversion from a relational and unconditional claim to a conditional one. The *imago Dei* has been quietly rewritten as a property that can be lost — that is forfeited by sufficiently serious wrongdoing, revoked by criminal behavior, suspended by judicial sentence.

But the text of *Genesis 1:27* contains no such qualification. It does not say: God created humankind in his image — *unless* they murder, *unless* they rape, *unless* they commit crimes of sufficient severity. The *tselem* does not depend on the behavior of the image. It depends on the act of the God who created it. And that act — the act of divine creation, of divine breathing-into, of divine loving into existence — is not revocable by human tribunal.

This is the precise theological claim that *John 19:11* makes in the context of Jesus's own execution. Standing before Pilate — who holds the formal legal authority to condemn him — Jesus says: *"You have no power over me unless it is given to you."* This is not a claim about Jesus's particular divinity only. It is a claim about the nature of all human power over human life: it is derived, borrowed, delegated — and it is therefore accountable to the one who delegated it. The state's power to punish is real. But it is not absolute. And the *imago Dei* is the theological limit of that power: the state may not execute what God has created in God's own image, because in doing so it destroys not merely a human life but the *tselem* of the God who made it.

This is the theological ground of the doctrinal position incorporated into *Catechism* 2267: the death penalty is *inadmissible* — not merely impractical, not merely disproportionate in most circumstances, but *inadmissible* — because it violates the inviolability of the *imago Dei*.

## PART III — THE SCRIPTURAL CONSTELLATION
*(9 minutes)*

I want to bring four scriptural texts into this argument — each adding a dimension the others do not supply, and together constituting a scriptural theology of the inviolability of human life.

**The first witness: Genesis 1:27 with Genesis 9:6.**

*Genesis 1:27* establishes the *imago Dei* as the ground of human dignity. But *Genesis 9:6* — the text most frequently cited in favour of capital punishment — requires careful attention: *"Whoever sheds the blood of a human, by a human shall that person's blood be shed; for in his own image God made humankind."*

The irony of this text is that it is deployed as a justification for capital punishment when its explicit theological rationale is the *imago Dei* — the very principle that, properly understood, argues against it. The text is saying: the reason for the gravity of taking human life is that every human being bears the divine image. The *imago Dei* is the reason murder is an offense of the highest order. And the Church's development of doctrine asks precisely this: if the *imago Dei* is the reason murder is so grave, how can the *imago Dei* be destroyed in the act of executing the murderer? You cannot simultaneously claim that human life is sacred because it bears the divine image — and then destroy that image in the name of justice.

**The second witness: Ezekiel 18:23.**

*"Have I any pleasure in the death of the wicked, says the Lord God, and not rather that they should turn from their ways and live?"*

This text is the most direct scriptural statement of the restorative theology of divine response to wrongdoing. God's pleasure — the theological term for what God desires, what God is oriented toward — is not the death of the wicked but their *metanoia* and their life. This is not a sentiment. It is a theological claim about the telos of divine justice: its goal is always the restoration of the person to the community of the living, not their elimination from it. Any human system of justice that takes as its final act the elimination of the person has diverged, at that point, from the telos of divine justice as Ezekiel names it.

**The third witness: John 8:2-11.**

The woman caught in adultery. The legal case is clear. The Torah's prescription is explicit — *Leviticus 20:10* mandates death. The crowd has gathered. The stones are ready. And Jesus bends down and writes in the dust.

Much has been written about what he wrote. I want to draw attention to what he did not do. He did not negotiate with the crowd. He did not suggest a lighter sentence. He did not argue that the prescribed penalty was disproportionate. He drew the crowd's attention to the moral continuity between themselves and the woman before them — *"Let anyone among you who is without sin be the first to throw a stone"* — and then, when they had left, he declined to execute the law that every legal and religious authority in the room agreed applied to her.

*"Neither do I condemn you."* This is not a statement about the woman's innocence. It is a statement about the purpose of the encounter with Jesus: not condemnation but restoration, not the execution of the law but the invitation to a new life. And it is a statement that carries an implicit Christological claim: the one who has the right to judge, declines to do so in the direction of death, and redirects in the direction of life.

**The fourth witness: John 10:10.**

*"I came that they may have life, and have it in fullness."*

This is Jesus's own self-description of his mission. Not: I came to distinguish the deserving from the undeserving and distribute life accordingly. Not: I came to ensure that consequences are proportionate to offenses. *I came that they may have life — in fullness.* The *zōē* of John's Gospel — the life Jesus offers — is not merely biological survival. It is life in its complete theological dimension: restored relationship with God, with the community, with oneself. It is the life that Ezekiel's God desires for every wicked person who turns. It is the life that the thief on the cross received in his final moments. It is the life that the father celebrated when the prodigal returned.

Capital punishment is the formal, legal, institutional declaration that *John 10:10* does not apply to this person. That this life — in fullness — is no longer a possibility for them. That the God who came to give life has, in this case, been overruled by the state.

The Church's doctrinal position, expressed in *Catechism* 2267, is simply the formal theological refusal of that declaration.

## PART IV — THE HISTORICAL DIAGNOSIS AND ITS INDIAN CONSEQUENCE
*(8 minutes)*

The historical diagnosis I want to offer is this: the Church's toleration — and in some periods, active endorsement — of capital punishment was a consequence of the same post-Constantinian theological compromise I identified in our third reflection.

When the Church became the established religion of the Roman Empire in the fourth century, it progressively inherited the Empire's legal and penal logic — including the retributive logic of Roman law. Aquinas, following Augustine, argued that the state had the right to execute criminals for the common good, as a diseased limb is amputated from the body for the health of the whole. This position was not without theological seriousness. But it was a position formed within, and accommodating itself to, the logic of a state that the Church was no longer challenging from outside but inhabiting from within.

The development of doctrine that Pope Francis incorporated into *Catechism* 2267 is not a reversal of the tradition. It is, as the Catechism itself explains, a development: *"an increasing awareness that the dignity of the person is not lost even after the commission of very serious crimes."* The *imago Dei* was always there. The full force of its implications — that the dignity of the criminal is inviolable, that the state's power over human life is not absolute — was not fully drawn out until the twentieth and twenty-first centuries' deepening engagement with human rights theology.

Cardinal Avery Dulles, in his analysis of the death penalty question, acknowledged this development honestly: the tradition was not uniform, and the direction of serious theological reflection in the contemporary period has been consistently away from retributive justifications for capital punishment and toward the position now formally incorporated into the Catechism. This is not Francis imposing a personal opinion. It is the formal recognition of a development that the best theological reflection of the last century had been moving toward.

Now, the Indian consequence of this diagnosis must be stated in three registers.

First: the Indian Penal Code's provision for capital punishment is a colonial inheritance — written by Lord Macaulay's commission in 1860, imposed by the British on a colonized people, and subsequently abandoned by Britain itself in 1969. We are using a penal instrument that the colonizers found incompatible with a civilized society and removed from their own legal system — as if it were a native Indian value. The post-colonial consciousness that shaped independent India's self-understanding should have included, as a matter of intellectual consistency, the interrogation of this colonial legal inheritance. That it has not is itself a cultural and political phenomenon worth examining.

Second: the phenomenon of mob violence and lynching — which has become, in contemporary India, a disturbing feature of public life — is capital punishment without a court. It is the retributive logic enacted without the procedural restraints of due process, without evidence, without a defense, without the theoretical possibility of acquittal. A person is accused — of theft, of transporting a particular food item across a state border, of belonging to the wrong religious community — and killed by a crowd that has appointed itself judge, jury, and executioner. The crowd films it. Sometimes political figures praise it. The *imago Dei* is destroyed in public, in daylight, with collective enthusiasm.

The Church cannot be silent about this. The silence of religious communities in the face of this phenomenon — in a country where Christians are themselves among the targets of communal mob violence, in Manipur, in Odisha, in Kandhamal — is not merely a moral failure. It is a failure of theological nerve: the failure to apply, in the public sphere, a doctrine that has been formally incorporated into the Catechism.

Third: Sr Inigo's concept of the *"hidden death penalty"* requires the most serious attention from every formation community in this region. Life imprisonment under conditions of maximum security — solitary confinement, permanent incarceration without possibility of parole, the systematic removal of every reason to hope — is, as Pope Francis explicitly stated, structurally not different from the death penalty. It is death administered slowly. And the psychological literature on its effects is unambiguous: paranoia, severe depression, chronic deterioration of mental and physical health, and — frequently — suicide.

But the hidden death penalty does not operate only in prisons. It operates — and this is the question Sr Inigo asks every community to sit with — in religious houses where a person has been judged, written off, excluded from community life, deprived of recognition and relationship, crushed by the accumulated weight of institutional contempt. The novice who has been dismissed as unsuitable and treated accordingly for three years before the formal dismissal. The older member who has been rendered invisible by the community's indifference. The person whose mistake became their permanent identity in the community's collective memory.

These are not equivalent to judicial execution. But they participate in the same spiritual logic: the logic that says *this person is done, has no future, is beyond redemption, is no longer worth the investment of attention and care.* And that logic — wherever it operates — is a theological error. Because the God who said *"I came that they may have life — in fullness"* did not except the person in the cell, or the person in the back room of the convent, or the person in the seminary whose name the Rector has already mentally crossed off the list.

## PROPHETIC DEMAND & SILENCE
*(3 minutes)*

Let me close with a demand — and then with the silence it requires.

There is a detail about the crucifixion that the Church has meditated on for two thousand years without drawing its full consequence. Jesus was executed by the state. Legally. Officially. With the procedural authority of Roman judicial power. The sentence was carried out correctly. And the sentence was wrong. The most famous execution in history was a judicial murder. And the one executed was the Son of God.

Which means that when we look at the cross — the symbol we wear, the image before which we pray, the sign beneath which we profess our vows — we are looking at what state execution did to God.

We follow a God who was killed by a government, buried in borrowed earth, and raised to vindicate every human being the world has discarded as beyond hope.

Until and unless consecrated persons in India are willing to preach, form, and advocate in accordance with *Catechism* 2267 — until the Church's formal doctrinal position on the inadmissibility of capital punishment is as present in our pastoral practice as our positions on matters far less central to the Gospel — our advocacy for human dignity will remain selective, and therefore finally incredible.

The question I leave with you is one:

**In your ministry setting, what would it cost you — institutionally, socially, politically — to preach and act consistently with the Church's formal teaching that the death penalty is inadmissible and that every human person, without exception, bears the inviolable image of God? And are you willing to pay that cost?**

*(Sustained silence — minimum four minutes. Do not fill it.)*

## CLOSING PRAYER

*God of the *imago Dei* —
You pressed Your own form
into every human being You ever made.
Not the deserving ones.
Every one.

You breathed life into flesh
and called it very good —
and You have not revoked that breath,
not even for the ones
whose actions made us wish
You had.

We confess that we have been slow
to draw the full consequence of this.
We have preached Your image in theory
and stayed silent when it was destroyed in practice —
by the rope, by the lethal injection,
by the mob's fist,
by the life sentence that removes every reason to hope,
by the community's contempt
that kills without a weapon.

Forgive us the silence
that made us complicit.

Give us the courage
of the doctrine You have placed in our hands:
that every person in every cell
still carries Your sculptured form.
Still belongs to You.
Still has a future
that no court and no community
has the authority to revoke.

Let us be a Church
that protects life as you gave it —
unconditionally,
persistently,
at whatever cost to our own comfort and acceptability —
because the one we follow
was himself executed by the state,
and raised to vindicate
everything the state declared finished.

Amen.*

## SPEAKER NOTES

**Register:** Academic formation talk — the register of Fr. Joseph Lobo SJ. The theological argument must be rigorous and structurally coherent; the prophetic urgency must be unmistakable; the pastoral warmth enters in the scriptural section and the closing prayer. This is the talk with the most explicit political-doctrinal content in the series. The Lobo method of making the institutional Church's own documents do the prophetic work is particularly important here: the speaker is not expressing a personal liberal political opinion — they are presenting formally incorporated Catholic doctrine.

**Key theological moves:**

1. The **retributive vs. restorative theology** of punishment — with the structural theological error of the retributive model named precisely: it has no account of the future of the person, which means it is, at its limit, a theology of despair about the human person.
2. ***Imago Dei*** recovered etymologically — *tselem* as the sculptured figure asserting the king's presence, unconditional and irrevocable — showing that the *imago Dei* cannot be forfeited by criminal behavior without contradicting the very anthropological claim that makes murder an offense.
3. The **scriptural constellation**: Genesis 1:27 with 9:6 (the *imago Dei* as the reason capital punishment is self-contradictory), Ezekiel 18:23 (divine *telos* as *metanoia* and life, not death), John 8:2-11 (Jesus's explicit refusal of legal execution in the direction of restoration), John 10:10 (the mission of Jesus as the fullness of life for all).
4. The **historical diagnosis**: the post-Constantinian inheritance of Roman retributive legal logic; the colonial provenance of the Indian Penal Code's capital punishment provision; the development of doctrine in *Catechism* 2267 as a recovery, not a reversal.
5. The **three-fold Indian consequence**: the colonial inheritance of the IPC, mob violence and lynching as capital punishment without procedure, and the *"hidden death penalty"* within religious communities themselves.

**Church documents cited precisely:**
- *Catechism of the Catholic Church* 2267 (revised 2018): *"inadmissible... attack on the inviolability and dignity of the person"*
- Pope Francis: *"The death penalty is inadmissible... The Church works with determination for its abolition worldwide"*
- Pope Francis on life imprisonment: formally stated alongside the death penalty as structurally problematic

**Secular and theological thinkers deployed:**
- Cardinal Avery Dulles: honest acknowledgment of the tradition's non-uniformity and the direction of contemporary theological reflection (theological complement)
- Thomas Aquinas/Augustine: the tradition's prior position stated fairly before the development of doctrine is introduced — consistent with Lobo's practice of never caricaturing the position being critiqued
- Mahatma Gandhi: *"An eye for an eye makes the whole world blind"* — the retributive logic as the logic of the vendetta (Indian contextual complement)

**Pacing:**
- The Opening must establish both the doctrinal clarity (*Catechism* 2267) and the pastoral gap (the fact that it has been almost entirely unpreached) before the thesis is stated.
- Part I (Two Theologies) — the retributive model must be treated fairly before its structural error is named. The Lobo method never caricatures the position being critiqued.
- Part II (Etymological Recovery) — *tselem* as sculptured figure is the most theologically generative move in the talk. The argument that you cannot simultaneously ground the gravity of murder in the *imago Dei* and then destroy the *imago Dei* in the act of executing the murderer needs to be given time to land.
- Part III (Scriptural Constellation) — the Genesis 9:6 reading (the text most cited *for* capital punishment turned against it by its own theological rationale) is the sharpest exegetical moment. Do not rush it.
- Part IV (Indian Consequence) — the three registers must be stated with controlled sobriety. The hidden death penalty within religious communities is the most personally searching moment of the talk. Name it without hedging.
- The closing paragraph about the cross as state execution is the emotional and theological culmination. Slow down. Let it land completely before the demand.
- Four minutes of silence minimum after the demand.
