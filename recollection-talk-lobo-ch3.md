## OPENING: THE PROBLEMATIC OF PROPHETIC DOMESTICATION
*(7 minutes)*

By way of introducing this third and concluding reflection in the first part of our series, let me begin — as we have consistently begun — with a theological affirmation and then with the reality that contradicts it in observable and structurally locatable ways.

We say: the consecrated life is, by its nature, prophetic. The Second Vatican Council in *Lumen Gentium* 44 describes consecrated persons as *signa* — signs — of the Kingdom: visible, public, and necessarily counter-cultural testimonies to a reality that the dominant social order neither produces nor values. Pope Francis, in his address to the Union of Superiors General in November 2013, was characteristically direct: *"Religious are prophets."* Not devout people. Not professionally religious people. **Prophets.** And the prophetic function, as the entire biblical tradition understands it, is structurally and inevitably conflictual. Prophets do not exist in comfortable institutional relationships with the powers they are called to challenge.

That is the claim.

And now the reality.

Karl Rahner — whose theological stature in the Catholic tradition of the twentieth century is genuinely beyond dispute — looked at the institutional Church of his day and said something that should stop any serious person of faith cold. He observed that the Church's public face is dominated to a terrifying extent by ritualism, legalism, administration, and what he named *"a boring and resigned spiritual mediocrity continuing along familiar lines."*

Ritualism. Legalism. Administration. Mediocrity.

These are not the characteristics of a prophetic community. They are the characteristics of a domesticated one. And the question this reflection must confront — directly, theologically, without the consolation of institutional self-congratulation — is this: how did a community founded upon the witness of people who were stoned, beheaded, crucified, and burned alive for refusing to accommodate the dominant social order arrive at a condition accurately described by one of its greatest theologians as boring mediocrity?

The answer is not that the consecrated life has been inhabited by bad people. The answer is that it has undergone a specific, historically diagnosable process that I shall call *prophetic domestication* — a process by which the conflictual, costly, socially dangerous dimensions of prophetic vocation have been progressively absorbed into institutional life and rendered manageable, predictable, and harmless to the very powers the prophetic tradition was always designed to challenge.

In one sentence, if I were to state the thesis of this reflection: **The renewal of consecrated life as genuine prophetic witness requires not merely a recovery of prophetic language or sentiment, but a structural confrontation with the specific institutional mechanisms by which the prophetic vocation has been domesticated — mechanisms that are historically locatable, theologically nameable, and therefore, at least in principle, reversible.**

Let me develop this across four movements.

## PART I — TWO MODELS OF RELIGIOUS PRESENCE: CULTIC AND PROPHETIC
*(10 minutes)*

There are two models of religious presence that have coexisted, in tension, throughout the entire history of Christianity. The balance between them determines, more than any other single factor, whether a consecrated community is genuinely prophetic or merely institutionally religious.

I shall call them the *cultic model* and the *prophetic model*.

**The cultic model** understands the primary function of consecrated life as the maintenance and celebration of the sacred: the liturgy, the sacraments, the ordered rhythms of prayer, the spiritual care of the believing community. In this model, the religious is above all a person of God — set apart from the world, elevated above its immediate concerns, performing the sacred duties that the community of faith requires. The cultic model has genuine and indispensable theological legitimacy. The *ora* of the Benedictine *ora et labora* is not peripheral to the Church's life. A consecrated community that abandons contemplative prayer in favour of social activism has misunderstood the nature of prophetic witness as seriously as one that never moves beyond the chapel.

But the cultic model becomes a pathology — and this is the critical point — when ritual performance substitutes for moral transformation. When the celebration of the sacred becomes, in practice, a mechanism for avoiding the demands of the sacred. When the liturgy that was designed to form the community for mission becomes instead a protective enclosure that shields the community from the mission's cost.

This substitution has a long and precisely documented history in the biblical prophetic tradition. It is the pathology that Isaiah names in *Isaiah 1:11-17*, where God says: *"I have had enough of burnt offerings... I do not delight in the blood of bulls... Your hands are full of blood. Wash yourselves. Cease to do evil. Seek justice. Rescue the oppressed."* It is the pathology that Amos identifies in *Amos 5:21-24*, where God says: *"I hate, I despise your festivals... but let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream."* And it is the pathology that Jesus diagnoses in *Matthew 23:23-24*, where he says to the Pharisees: *"You tithe mint, dill, and cumin, but you have neglected the weightier matters of the law: justice, mercy, and faith."*

The prophetic tradition's critique of the cultic is not, I must be careful to say, a critique of worship as such. It is a critique of worship deployed as a substitute for justice — of the sacred instrumentalized as a refuge from the demands of the sacred.

**The prophetic model** of religious presence understands the primary function of consecrated life not as the maintenance of the sacred but as the *interruption* of the profane. The prophet does not stand apart from the world in order to be protected from it. The prophet stands at the precise intersection of the divine word and the human situation — and at that intersection, names what the dominant social order is unwilling to name, regardless of the institutional cost. Walter Brueggemann, in his analysis of prophetic imagination, identifies two constitutive functions of the prophetic: the *criticizing* of the dominant consciousness — naming what is wrong in the present arrangement of power and privilege — and the *energizing* of an alternative consciousness — opening, for the community, a vision of what God intends in its place.

Sr Inigo's account of the early Christian communities in *Acts 2:44-47* and *Acts 4:32-35* is an illustration of the prophetic model at its most coherent. These communities did not merely celebrate the Eucharist. They enacted it. They shared property, ate together across the social fault-lines of the Roman Empire — slave and master, Jew and Greek, man and woman at the same table — and created within the hostile social world of their day a visible, structurally embodied alternative. And the Empire's response to this — persecution, precisely because the community was genuinely dangerous to the social order — is itself the confirmation that the prophetic model was operating.

The Constantinian settlement of the fourth century was the decisive structural turning point. The granting of imperial favour, tax exemption, and political visibility to the Church was not merely a historical event. It was a structural temptation of the first order. And the Church, with honourable exceptions and against the protests of its prophetic voices in every subsequent century, largely accepted that temptation. Quality gave way to quantity. The radical community of equals was progressively replaced by a hierarchical institution that mirrored the social structures it was originally designed to challenge. The poor drifted from the centre of the mission to its margins. And the cultic model — manageable, prestigious, politically acceptable, administratively convenient — progressively domesticated the prophetic one.

This is not a counsel of despair about the institutional Church. But it is a condition of honest theological self-understanding. And the recovery of the prophetic model — which *Perfectae Caritatis* 2 gestures toward, which *Evangelii Gaudium* 20 insists upon, which the witness of modern martyrs embodies at the cost of blood — requires, first of all, that the domestication be named for what it is.

## PART II — ETYMOLOGICAL RECOVERY: WHAT *PROPHĒTĒS* ACTUALLY DEMANDS
*(8 minutes)*

Let me now perform the etymological recovery that this argument requires — returning a key word to its original verbal force before it was converted into a manageable institutional category.

The Greek *prophētēs* — from which the English *prophet* derives — is a compound: *pro* (before, or on behalf of) + *phēmi* (to speak). The *prophētēs* is one who speaks *pro*: either *in advance of* an event, or *on behalf of* a reality or a person who cannot adequately speak for themselves. The common rendering — the prophet as someone who predicts the future — captures the first meaning but almost entirely loses the second. And it is the second meaning that is theologically decisive.

The *prophētēs* in the biblical tradition is primarily the one who speaks *on behalf of* God — and speaks *on behalf of* those whose cause God's justice demands be heard. The Hebrew *nabi* — which the Septuagint renders as *prophētēs* — carries the additional resonance of one who is *borne along* by the divine word: not a person who chooses to speak as a matter of personal conviction, but a person who is compelled to speak, even against their own preference and comfort, because the word of God has become, in Jeremiah's devastating formulation, *"like a burning fire shut up in my bones"* (*Jeremiah 20:9*) that cannot finally be contained.

Now, what has happened to *prophētēs* in the dominant institutional self-understanding of consecrated life is precisely what happened to *consecrare* and *charisma* in the distortions we examined in our first two reflections: it has been converted from a **verb** into a **noun**. From a living, costly, publicly enacted vocation into an adjective of institutional self-description. **Prophetic** appears in congregational constitutions, mission statements, and chapter resolutions — without necessarily involving any of the structural conflict, social cost, or personal risk that the biblical tradition consistently and universally associates with the prophetic vocation.

A congregation can describe itself as prophetic in its documents while its actual institutional life is entirely accommodated to the dominant social and economic order. A religious can speak of their prophetic calling in retreat contexts while their daily life generates no discomfort whatsoever in the powerful — and no concrete solidarity whatsoever with the marginalized. This is *prophētēs* as noun: a category of self-understanding, a dimension of institutional identity, a label. What the biblical tradition demands is *prophētēs* as verb: a continuous, costly, structurally embodied orientation toward justice, enacted in specific public acts that carry genuine institutional and personal risk.

Abraham Heschel — whose analysis of the prophetic consciousness in the Hebrew tradition remains, in my judgment, the most theologically penetrating account available — makes a distinction that is essential here. He argues that the prophet is not someone who holds strong opinions about justice as a matter of personal ethical conviction. The prophet is someone in whom the divine *pathos* — God's passionate, suffering identification with the afflicted — has taken human form. The prophet is not arguing *about* justice from a position of external moral authority. The prophet is *suffering with* the one for whom justice has been denied, because the God who suffers with the afflicted has claimed the prophet's entire person for that solidarity.

This is why authentic prophetic witness is invariably recognizable by its cost — not by its rhetoric. The rhetorical dimension of prophecy is real, but secondary. The primary dimension is what I would call the *prophetic wager*: the willingness to stake one's institutional security, social acceptability, and potentially one's physical safety on the claim that the God of justice is real — and that this reality demands specific, non-negotiable, embodied action here, among these people, in this social situation, now.

## PART III — THE SCRIPTURAL CONSTELLATION: THE SIX CONFLICTS OF JESUS
*(10 minutes)*

I want to build the scriptural argument around a single, structurally important theological observation: **Jesus was not crucified by accident or by misunderstanding.** He was crucified as the direct, traceable consequence of specific, repeated, deliberately chosen confrontations with the institutional powers of his day. And if the prophetic vocation is — as *Ad Gentes* 5 explicitly affirms — a participation in the mission of Jesus, then those confrontations are not merely historical data about a first-century Palestinian teacher. They are a structural map of what prophetic witness entails in every social and ecclesial context.

Sr Inigo identifies six conflict-zones in the ministry of Jesus. I want to engage each theologically, attending to the detail that most readings pass over.

**The first conflict: with the religious leadership.**

*Matthew 23:13-33* — the seven *"woes"* addressed publicly to the scribes and Pharisees — is the most sustained confrontational speech in the entire Synoptic tradition. Seven times, in the Temple precincts, in the presence of the crowds: *"Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites."* The Greek *hypokritēs* means, literally, one who plays a role — an actor whose public persona bears no correspondence to their actual person.

The prophetic charge is not that the scribes and Pharisees are wicked. It is that they have perfected the performance of religion — with precision, with institutional visibility, with social credibility — while evacuating from that performance its substance: *"justice, mercy, and faith"* (*Matthew 23:23*). The whitewashed tomb is not an image of malice. It is an image of dissociation: a carefully maintained exterior bearing no relationship to the interior reality it is supposed to represent.

The question this text puts to every religious community is not comfortable: what is the actual relationship, in our institutional life, between the exterior — the habit, the timetable, the liturgical calendar, the reputation — and the interior, which is the concrete condition of our engagement with the poor, the marginalized, and the truth?

**The second conflict: with political authority.**

Before Pilate (*Matthew 27:13-14*), Jesus does not negotiate. Before Herod (*Luke 23:9*), he maintains sustained silence. These two responses — the brief refusal of negotiation and the deliberate silence — are not passivity. They are prophetic acts. Dietrich Bonhoeffer, who understood political authority and prophetic response at a cost most of us will never face, said that there are moments when the only authentically Christian response is to refuse the terms that the powerful have set — not to argue within their framework, but to stand outside it entirely, bearing witness to a legitimacy that their authority cannot confer or revoke.

**The third conflict: with tradition.**

The Sabbath healings (*John 5:15-16*), the disciples eating without ritual handwashing (*Mark 7:5-9*), the touching of the leper — these are not instances of casual irreverence toward received tradition. They are deliberate, precisely targeted theological acts. Each one embodies a principle that *Mark 2:27* states explicitly: *"The Sabbath was made for humankind, and not humankind for the Sabbath."* Human dignity is the criterion by which every traditional practice must be evaluated. When a tradition — liturgical, canonical, institutional — functions to preserve an arrangement of privilege rather than to serve human flourishing, the prophetic vocation requires its critique. Not its abandonment — its critique. The prophet does not discard tradition. The prophet holds tradition accountable to its own deepest intention.

**The fourth conflict: with his immediate community.**

*John 6:52, 66* — after the Bread of Life discourse: *"many of his disciples drew back and no longer went about with him."* *Mark 14:50* — at the arrest in Gethsemane: *"all his disciples deserted him and fled."* The people Jesus had formed, invested in, walked with — at the hour of prophetic cost, they were absent.

Henri Nouwen, in his analysis of the *"wounded healer,"* observes that the prophetic person's most characteristic and deepest wound is almost always the wound of abandonment by the very community that was supposed to share the mission. This is not a reason to abandon the community. But it is a reason to locate one's ultimate grounding not in communal solidarity — which is real and necessary but always, in the end, fragile — but in the relationship with God that the community is meant to express and serve.

**The fifth conflict: with his own family.**

*Mark 3:21* — *"his family went out to restrain him, for people were saying, 'He has gone out of his mind.'"* The people who knew Jesus longest, loved him most specifically, and had the greatest personal investment in his safety — they were the ones who concluded that he had lost his judgment. The prophetic vocation regularly produces this form of opposition: the opposition of those who love you and are therefore most threatened by what the vocation is costing them. In the Indian formation context — where family bonds are not merely affective but structurally central to social life and social honour — this dimension of the prophetic cost is particularly acute and must be named rather than evaded.

**The sixth conflict: with God.**

*Matthew 27:46* — *"My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?"* — the opening verse of *Psalm 22*, spoken from the cross. This is the deepest conflict in the prophetic life: the moment when the very God on whose authority the prophet has staked everything appears to be absent — precisely when the prophet has paid the full cost of fidelity.

It is essential to say what this text is and is not. It is not a text about theological doubt in the abstract. It is a text about the experience — from within a dying human body — of the apparent silence of the God who was always supposed to be present. And its theological weight is that Jesus has spoken it. The prophetic vocation does not protect the prophet from the experience of divine absence. It offers, instead, a tradition of honest witness within which that experience can be held — not resolved, not explained away, but held — in the context of a life genuinely oriented toward God.

The Resurrection is not an explanation of *Matthew 27:46*. It is the presence of the one who asked the question, on the other side of what appeared to be its definitive and final answer. That has always been enough. It is, in fact, the only thing that makes the prophetic wager genuinely possible.

## PART IV — FOUR STRUCTURAL SUPPRESSORS AND THE COST OF PROPHETIC WITNESS
*(7 minutes)*

If the prophetic vocation is genuine — if it is, as I have argued, not merely a dimension of institutional self-description but a structurally embodied, personally costly orientation — then we must ask: what are the specific institutional mechanisms by which it is being suppressed in our congregations today? Sr Inigo identifies four. I want to name each one in theological register.

**The first suppressor: the culture of informed silence.**

We live in a moment of unprecedented informational access. The bonded labourer in Murshidabad, the trafficked girl from Malda, the adivasi community in Purulia watching their forest disappear, the undertrial prisoner in Dum Dum Jail who has been waiting seven years for a hearing — their situations are documented. They appear in reports, in WhatsApp forwards, in newspaper articles accessible to anyone with a phone.

And the response of most religious communities to this information is — silence. Not ignorance. Silence. A carefully maintained, institutionally protective, reputationally cautious silence.

Albert Einstein, from a very different discipline, identified the mechanism precisely: the world, he said, will not be destroyed by those who do evil, but by those who watch them without doing anything. In the theological register, silence in the context of documentable injustice is not a neutral absence. It is a choice. And it is a choice with a theological name: complicity.

**The second suppressor: the logic of institutionalisation.**

I have addressed this in our previous two reflections, but it requires restating in the prophetic register. The institution — the school, the hospital, the social centre — was built to serve the mission. Over time, through the structural logic that Edward Schillebeeckx identified as the *hardening* of charism, the relationship reverses: the mission is made to serve the institution. Decisions are made not by asking *"What does the God of justice require of us here?"* but by asking *"What will protect our registration, our land, our annual returns?"*

A mission that has reorganized itself to protect its institutional interest has, in the most precise theological sense, lost the mission. It retains the form. It no longer inhabits the substance.

**The third suppressor: the culture of internal domination.**

Sr Inigo names this suppressor with what I consider the talk's most courageous observation: dominant cultural groups within congregations impose their way of being on minority members, producing consequences she calls *"disastrous and insensitive."* The Hindi-speaking formation house that marginalizes the Bengali-speaking candidate. The urban community that dismisses the rural formation candidate as insufficiently sophisticated. The senior member who treats the junior not as a companion in mission but as a subordinate to be managed.

The theological consequence is not merely a community relations problem. A congregation that practices cultural elitism within its walls while speaking prophetically about caste discrimination in the street has produced a contradiction that will, in time, discredit both the internal practice and the external witness. Prophetic integrity is not divisible. You cannot be prophetic outside the convent and hierarchical within it.

**The fourth suppressor: the culture of individualism and disposability.**

The convergence of social media culture — the performance of self, the carefully curated institutional identity — with the broader throwaway culture that discards goods, relationships, and people when they cease to be useful, produces a spiritual posture that is, in its deepest structure, incompatible with prophetic vocation. The throwaway culture is not merely an ethical failure. It is, as Pope Francis argues in *Evangelii Gaudium* 53, the concrete social expression of a market logic that assigns value to human beings by their productive utility — a logic that is, in the most direct sense, the logic of anti-prophecy. The prophetic tradition is built upon the infinite, non-negotiable value of the human person before God — a value that no market, no institution, no social utility can confer or revoke.

Now, the cost of resisting these suppressors. Sr Inigo names the modern martyrs — Romero, Rani Maria, Valsa John, the martyrs of Kandhamal — not as exceptional figures whose witness is beyond ordinary emulation, but as the visible tip of a prophetic cost that every serious follower of Jesus is asked to pay in less dramatic but no less real forms. Not every prophet sheds blood. But every prophet sheds *something*: reputation, security, institutional advancement, the approval of the powerful, the comfort of accommodation.

Tertullian, in the third century, watching the martyrs die, observed that the blood of the martyrs is the seed of the Church. The principle holds beyond physical martyrdom. Every act of genuine prophetic witness — every decision to name what must be named, to stand where the institution finds it inconvenient to stand, to be present with those the dominant social order renders invisible — plants something. It does not always flower in the planter's lifetime. It does not need to. The prophetic vocation has never required visible results as its justification.

The justification of prophetic witness is the reality of the God who sends it.

## PROPHETIC DEMAND & SILENCE
*(3 minutes)*

Let me close this third reflection — and with it the first part of our series — with a demand rather than a consolation.

We have walked, across three reflections, a single theological arc: from the crisis of mission (*"Am I still alive?"*), through the crisis of identity (*"Do I know who I am?"*), to the crisis of courage (*"Am I genuinely prophetic — or have I been domesticated?"*). These are not three separate questions. They are three dimensions of a single question about the integrity of consecrated life. You cannot be prophetic without being genuinely rooted in your charism. You cannot be genuinely rooted in your charism without being alive to the God who gave it.

The text of 2 Kings 17:13 — *"Turn from your evil ways"* — is addressed in its original context not to the wicked, the apostate, or the enemies of Israel. It is addressed to God's own people, who had built the institutions, performed the rituals, and maintained the forms — while losing the substance that gave the forms their meaning. The prophets warned them. They did not listen. And the consequences were institutional, social, and spiritual collapse.

The parallel is not comfortable. It is, however, precise.

The question I leave with you is this — one question, for this afternoon's prayer and tonight's silence:

**What is the specific act of prophetic witness that your social context, your ministry setting, and your congregation's charism require of you — and what is the specific cost you have been unwilling to pay?**

*(Sustained silence — minimum four minutes. Do not fill it.)*

## CLOSING PRAYER

*God of Jeremiah, God of Amos,
God of the ones who were driven to the brow of the hill
and led to the edge —

You have always needed people willing
to say what others would not say,
to stand where others would not stand,
to remain present where others
found reasons to be elsewhere.

We confess that we have, in many of our choices,
preferred the cultic to the prophetic:
the beautiful liturgy over the costly solidarity,
the institutional reputation over the inconvenient truth,
the managed charity over the dangerous justice.

Forgive us not with words
but with the same burning fire
You shut up in Jeremiah's bones.

Make us inconvenient.
Make us structurally uncomfortable
to the arrangements that keep the poor poor
and the powerful powerful
and the silence of complicity
mistaken for the peace of God.

Give us the courage of Romero,
who knew what was coming and said Mass anyway.
Give us the fidelity of Rani Maria,
who organized the powerless
even when the powerful came for her on a bus.
Give us the clarity of John Chrysostom,
who knew that the tabernacle and the beggar at the door
are not two separate theological problems.

In West Bengal — in Murshidabad, in Purulia, in Malda,
in the corridors of Dum Dum Jail,
in the formation houses where the next generation of prophets
is either being formed or being domesticated —

Let us be found on the right side.
Not the comfortable side.
The right side.

Amen.*

1. The **cultic model vs. prophetic model** of religious presence — with the Constantinian settlement named as the historical turning point at which the balance shifted decisively.
