## 🔷 OPENING PROBLEMATIC — THE GAP WE MUST NAME (7 minutes)

By way of introducing this talk, let me begin with a well-known problematic.

We say that Christian education is a mission. We place the word "mission" in our school's prospectus, in our congregation's constitutions, in every speech delivered on Founder's Day. We say that our schools exist *for the poor*, that they are the continuation of the founding charism, that they bear witness to the Gospel of Jesus Christ in the marketplace of learning.

But the lived reality shows us something different.

In Catholic schools across India today — and this is not a polemical assertion; it is a documented statistic that Sr Inigo places before us in Chapter 12 — more than fifty percent of the student body comes from well-off families. In a country where the vast majority of people live below or near the poverty line, the schools founded explicitly for the marginalized now primarily serve those who were never in danger of marginalization.

Let me be precise about what this means, because the consequences follow with logical necessity.

*First*: the founding charism has been inverted. The congregations that built these schools did not build them for the children of the elite. They built them facing the adivasi villages of Jharkhand, the Dalit communities of Tamil Nadu, the landless laborers of Bengal. The inversion did not happen through malice. It happened gradually, through understandable financial pressures, through the logic of prestige, through the gravitational pull of institutional sustainability. But the result is inversion nonetheless.

*Second*: the theological claim embedded in the word "mission" has been hollowed out. A mission directed primarily toward the already-privileged is not a Christian mission in any sense that the Gospel recognizes. Pope Francis, in *Evangelii Gaudium* 49, is unambiguous: "I want a Church which is poor and for the poor." What applies to the Church applies structurally to every institution that calls itself an instrument of the Church's mission.

*Third*: the students produced by such institutions — and this is Sr Inigo's most uncomfortable empirical observation — include many of the most powerful and corrupt actors in Indian public life. Former students of Catholic schools who today vote for legislation that persecutes Christians. Former alumni who remain silent when missionaries are attacked. Former products of Catholic formation who participate with full social ease in systems of caste discrimination and economic exploitation.

Something has gone wrong — not only with what these schools teach, but with what they form.

In one sentence, if I were to respond to this problematic: *Christian education becomes a commercial venture, and ceases to be an apostolate, at the precise moment when it begins to optimize for the student who already has everything rather than for the student who has nothing.*

The rest of this talk is an unpacking of that sentence — historically, theologically, scripturally, and institutionally.

## 🔷 PART 1 — EDUCATION: MISSION OR VERB THAT BECAME A NOUN? (9 minutes)

Let me begin with a linguistic observation — and I ask you to stay with it, because it carries the entire structural diagnosis.

The Second Vatican Council's *Gravissimum Educationis* opens with a remarkable formulation. It speaks of education not as a service that institutions render, but as a *right* — a genuine right of every human person by virtue of being human. The Latin is precise: *vera educatio* — "true education" — is defined there in article 1 as the formation of a person toward "the ultimate goal of the human person, and at the same time toward the good of the societies of which the human being is a member."

Notice what this is. Education is a *relational act* between a person and their ultimate destiny, mediated through community. It is, theologically, a *participation in the creative and redemptive work of God.*

Now. What has happened to this understanding in the way our Catholic institutions actually operate?

*Educating* — the active verb, the relational act, the participation in God's redemptive purpose — has been reduced to *education* — the noun, the product, the credential, the commodity.

This is the same movement that Fr. Lobo has traced in other theological concepts. *Eucharistein* — the active verb of thanksgiving and self-offering — became "the Eucharist" — the noun, the ceremony, the ritual to be attended. *Leitourgia* — the public works of the people for the common good — became "liturgy" — the private cultic exercise of a gathered community. And now: *educating* — the active, prophetic, liberating act of forming conscience and empowering the powerless — has become "education" — the product sold at premium rates to families who can afford the fee receipt.

This is not merely a linguistic observation. Every verb-to-noun conversion produces real, observable institutional consequences.

When education is a *verb*, the central question is: *Who needs to be educated, and what must I do to reach them?* The school goes to the child. The congregation takes its mission to the tea garden, to the adivasi belt, to the flood-plain community in the Sundarbans. The institution bears the cost of mission.

When education is a *noun*, the central question is: *What is the quality of the product we are offering, and who will pay for it?* The child must come to the school. The child must have the fee receipt. The child must have the uniform, the academic baseline, the cultural fluency to navigate the institution's social codes. If the child does not have these things, the institution is not for them.

Victor Hugo's famous line — "when you open a school door, you close a prison door" — is a profound statement about education as verb. But it only functions theologically if the school's door is facing the child who would otherwise end up in the prison. If the door has been turned around to face the child who was never realistically going to end up there, then Victor Hugo's statement becomes a slogan on a wall.

Mother Teresa — who ran schools, and understood them from the inside — said something that should be placed alongside Victor Hugo: "Don't leave your schools. But change your strategies." Not: close your schools. Not: abandon the institutions. But: *change* — specifically, structurally, with honest self-examination — *the strategies.*

And changing strategies means, first of all, recovering the verb.

## 🔷 PART 2 — THE SCRIPTURAL CONSTELLATION: WHO IS THE SCHOOL FOR? (9 minutes)

Now let me come to the scriptural case — and I ask you to notice how these texts form a constellation, each one adding a dimension that the others do not carry alone.

**Hebrews 5:12** is our first anchor: *"You need someone to teach you."*

The context is significant, and I do not want us to read past it. The author of Hebrews is addressing a community that has been Christians long enough to be teachers — and is instead still requiring to be taught the rudiments. The word used for "elements" is *stoicheia* — basic building blocks, the foundation stones of a structure. The author is saying: you should be building by now. You are still mixing cement.

But behind the specific pastoral frustration is a universal theological claim: *every human person, at every stage of development, has the inalienable right and need to be taught.* Not the privileged person. Not the academically promising person. Not the person whose parents can afford the fee receipt. *Every person.* The word in the Greek is *tines* — *some of you* in the immediate pastoral reference, but the underlying anthropology is universal.

*"You need someone to teach you."* The question our institutions must answer honestly is: are we that someone? And for whom?

**John 10:10** is our second text: *"I have come that they may have life — life in all its fullness."*

The Greek is *zoe* — not *bios* (biological existence) but the full, qualitative, relational life of God's children. And the word *perisson* — rendered "in all its fullness" — carries the sense of abundance, of overflow, of life beyond the minimum.

Notice whom Jesus is addressing in John 10. He is not in the Jerusalem Temple, speaking to the educated and the powerful. He is addressing people who have been described in the preceding chapters as the *ochlos* — the crowd, the unnamed, the socially undifferentiated mass of ordinary people. The blind man who was thrown out of the synagogue in chapter 9. The woman taken in public disgrace. The person whom the religious institution has excluded.

*Zoe perisson* — abundant life — is the offer Jesus makes precisely to those whom the institutions have excluded. If our schools are primarily serving those who already have *bios* — comfortable biological existence, social security, economic stability — and are not reaching those for whom *zoe perisson* would be genuinely transformative, then we are not continuing the mission of John 10:10. We are running a very respectable service organization.

**Matthew 25:31–46** is our third and most uncomfortable text.

The judgment scene. You know the text. But I want to draw your attention to a detail that is easy to read past.

The criteria of judgment in Matthew 25 are entirely relational and concrete: food for the hungry, water for the thirsty, welcome for the stranger, clothing for the naked, visit to the sick and the imprisoned. These are not liturgical criteria. They are not doctrinal criteria. They are not academic criteria.

And the most theologically dense moment in the passage is verse 44: *"Lord, when did we see you hungry, or thirsty, or a stranger, or naked, or sick, or in prison, and did not minister to you?"*

The question from those on the left hand — *when did we see you?* — reveals the structural sin. They did not fail to serve Christ because they were evil. They failed to see Christ because their entire framework of attention was oriented elsewhere. They were looking in the wrong direction.

The child at the gate without a fee receipt is the one in whom, as Matthew 25:40 tells us, Christ is present: *"as you did it to one of the least of these my brothers and sisters, you did it to me."* The question our institutions must answer is not only what we do for the child after we let them in. The question is whether our institutional gaze is capable of recognizing Christ in the child we have turned away.

**Isaiah 10:1–2** gives us the prophetic diagnosis of what happens when institutions of power stop serving the vulnerable: *"Woe to those who make unjust laws, to those who issue oppressive decrees, to deprive the poor of their rights and withhold justice from the oppressed of my people, making widows their prey and robbing the fatherless."*

The prophet Isaiah is not speaking of a school. But the structural logic transfers with precision. An institution that was constituted to serve the vulnerable — and that, through accumulated policies of fee structure, admission criteria, and aesthetic preference, has systematically excluded the vulnerable — is, in Isaiah's vocabulary, an institution that has made "unjust laws." The fees policy. The admission criteria. The preference for students who will not affect the rank lists. These are the *decrees* that Isaiah is naming.

The prophetic tradition does not allow institutions to hide behind good intentions. It judges by consequence.

## 🔷 PART 3 — THE HISTORICAL DIAGNOSIS: WHEN DID THE REVERSAL HAPPEN? (8 minutes)

Fr. Lobo's method — and it is a method we need to apply here — insists on identifying precisely *when* a theological distortion entered the tradition. This is not nostalgia. It is diagnosis. And diagnosis requires locating the specific point of inversion.

The reversal in Christian education did not happen in a single moment. But it has an identifiable logic, and that logic can be traced.

In the early period of the missionary congregations in India — the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries — the schools built by religious were genuinely frontier institutions. Sr. Inigo's chapter documents this. They were built in places that had no other educational infrastructure. The founding Sisters, Brothers, and Fathers went where no one else was going. The first students were precisely the children of the poor: tribal children, Dalit children, children of displaced laborers, children of fishing communities.

The reversal began with what sociologists of religion call the "institutionalization of charisma" — a concept that Max Weber analyzed in careful sociological terms. When a charismatic movement becomes an institution, the original drive that created it must be translated into policies, structures, and systems that can survive the death of the founders. And in that process of translation, something is always lost: the willingness to bear the institutional cost of the founding vision.

The founding vision of Catholic education in India was costly. It required going to where there was no infrastructure, no fees, no economic return. It required investing in children whose families could offer nothing in return. It required a long-term, multigenerational patience.

When the institutions became successful — when the schools became known for quality — a new pressure entered: the pressure of financial sustainability. Buildings required maintenance. Teachers required salaries. Government regulations required compliance costs. And the most efficient way to meet these costs was to admit students whose families could pay.

The first fee increase was not a betrayal. It was an adjustment. The second fee increase was still defensible. By the third generation of adjustments, the admission profile had shifted substantially. By the time the profile had shifted beyond fifty percent toward well-off families, no single decision had been the turning point — but the cumulative effect was inversion.

This is what Walter Brueggemann calls "the embrace of royal consciousness" — when the prophetic imagination of a movement surrenders to the administrative logic of the establishment. Brueggemann, in his *The Prophetic Imagination*, argues that the royal consciousness "achieves amnesia" regarding the founding commitment. It does not renounce the original vision. It simply stops seeing it as urgent. The language of the founding charism survives in the prospectus and on the school crest. The practice has moved entirely elsewhere.

The National Consultation on Catholic Education in India in 2004 named this clearly: education is a mission, but not every type of education is the Christian mission. If our schools empower the already empowered — we are running a profession. We are not running an apostolate.

And this distinction matters with theological precision. A profession serves those who come to it on the profession's terms. An apostolate goes to those who cannot come on any terms at all — and changes its own structure in order to reach them.

## 🔷 PART 4 — FOUR INSTITUTIONAL MYTHS AND THE THEOLOGICAL RESPONSE (9 minutes)

Now. Having established the problematic, the scriptural case, and the historical diagnosis — I want to address directly the four myths that are used, consciously or unconsciously, to defend the current institutional situation. I call them myths because they have the internal logic of experience, they feel like truths, and they are theologically inadequate.

**Myth One: "Poor children cannot cope with a good standard — especially English medium."**

This is the myth that sounds most compassionate and is, in practice, most corrosive. Because what it is actually asserting is that the incapacity is located inside the child rather than in the quality of teaching the child receives.

*Gravissimum Educationis* 1 speaks of every child's right to education that is "adapted to their particular destiny." Not education adapted to institutional convenience. *Their* particular destiny. This is the theological starting point. If the child is not learning, the first question is not "Can this child learn?" The first question is "Are we teaching adequately?"

The insight of experienced educators working with marginalized communities is consistent: given adequate remedial support, consistent investment, and the expectation of success — children from any background can learn. The failure is pedagogical and institutional. It is not located in the child.

**Myth Two: "Good academic results signify a good school."**

The state board ranking and the Gospel ranking are not the same list. *Gravissimum Educationis* 8 calls for education that forms not only the academic capacity but "a genuine sense of responsibility" and the disposition to "contribute to the common good." A school that consistently produces rank-holders who are, as adults, morally asleep — comfortable, entitled, and socially indifferent — has achieved an academic result and failed a theological one.

The question Sr Inigo asks in Chapter 12 is the question every school governing board should be required to answer annually: what is the outcome of our ministry? Not: how many students passed the board examination. Not: how many scholarships did our students receive. But: what kind of people did they become? Did they become the social reformers that Matthew 25 requires? Or did they become successful professionals who have been equipped to join the system rather than to dismantle it?

**Myth Three: "The poor will dirty the place and disrupt discipline."**

I want to be precise about this myth, because it is almost never stated aloud and is frequently the operating assumption behind admission decisions.

There is a well-documented phenomenon in educational sociology: when institutions begin associating the presence of poor students with a decline in institutional "culture," what they are actually expressing is a preference for the aesthetic and behavioral norms of the already-privileged. The child who arrives without cultural fluency, without the behavioral codes that middle-class schools valorize, without the quiet confidence that comes from a stable home environment — that child is read as a disruption. But theologically: *that child is Christ.*

Matthew 25:40. I do not need to repeat it.

Sr Cyril of Kolkata — whose educational mission for the poor in this city was one of the most remarkable of the twentieth century — said something that should silence this myth: "If you want silence, go to a graveyard. There you will have perfect silence." A school that achieves its quiet order by excluding the children of the poor is not well-governed. It is curated. And the curation is a theological failure.

**Myth Four: "The poor cannot afford the fees, and the fees are necessary for institutional sustainability."**

This is the most structurally serious myth, because it contains a genuine financial reality. But Sr Inigo's response is institutional and creative: build *corpus funds*; seek *benefactors specifically for this purpose*; examine where resources are currently being spent.

And here is the institutional examination she calls for: compare the money spent on the new tiled corridor, the freshly painted gate, the impressive signboard — with the cost of twenty scholarship seats. The newly tiled corridor is not wrong in itself. It becomes a theological problem when it consumes resources that could have paid the fees of children who are currently standing outside the gate.

*Gaudium et Spes* 69 speaks of the universal destination of goods — the principle that the goods of creation are meant for all, and that private ownership and institutional resources carry a social mortgage. Applied to our institutions: the building belongs to the mission. The mission is the child at the gate.

## 🔷 PART 5 — RE-VISIONING: THE PROPHETIC DEMAND (8 minutes)

I want to come now to what Sr Inigo calls "re-visioning" — and I want to be very precise about what this means and what it does not mean.

Re-visioning does not mean closing Catholic schools. Mother Teresa was right: "Don't leave your schools." The schools are needed. In the rural communities of North Bengal, in the adivasi belts of Purulia and Jharkhand, in the Sundarbans flood communities, in the urban slums of Kolkata — the institutional presence of the Church in education is irreplaceable. There is no argument here for withdrawal.

Re-visioning means three things, stated with the precision they require.

*First: a structural change in admission policy.* The goal is not a token scholarship programme. The goal is a fundamental reorientation of what the school exists for. This means setting a specific, measurable, annually reviewed percentage of students from marginalized communities — and holding the institution accountable to that percentage. It means building in the support structures (remedial coaching, nutrition support, counseling, fee waiver mechanisms) that make genuine inclusion rather than nominal admission possible.

*Second: an honest institutional examination of the laity question.* Sr Inigo raises something structurally important and institutionally uncomfortable: lay teachers and lay principals are, in many cases, better equipped than religious for the sustained daily work of school administration. They have more undivided professional attention. They are not carrying simultaneous responsibilities of community life, formation, and canonical obligation. Her proposal — bold, and I think theologically sound — is this: *elevate talented lay people to leadership roles in Catholic schools, and free consecrated persons to move to the frontier ministries that no one else is doing.*

The prison. The trafficking corridor. The adivasi community with no institutional Church presence. The ministry of accompaniment in the slum. These are not being done because the consecrated persons who could do them are managing institutions that a well-formed laity could manage with equal competence and greater sustainability.

*Gravissimum Educationis* 8 already anticipated this: it speaks of the role of the laity in Catholic education not as auxiliary but as genuinely constitutive. The post-conciliar Church has been slow to act on this. Sixty years after the Council, religious superiors still resist handing governance of schools to laypeople because the institutional trust has not been built. Building that trust is not a concession. It is a mission priority.

*Third: a re-orientation of what the school measures as success.* Pope Francis, in *Evangelii Gaudium* 49, writes of the Church's preferential option for the poor as not merely programmatic but hermeneutical — it is the *lens* through which we read everything. Applied to education: the preferential option for the poor is not a scholarship programme added to an existing system. It is the criterion by which every institutional decision is read. *Does this decision bring us closer to the child who has nothing? Or does it take us further away?*

Apply that criterion to the last five major decisions of any Catholic school governing board. The fee revision. The new construction project. The capital allocation. The admission criteria for next year. The appointment to a leadership role.

*Does this bring us closer to the child at the gate?*

That is the question. It is not complicated. It is costly. And the cost is what distinguishes a mission from a profession.

The post-COVID moment is, as Sr Inigo argues, an unexpected opening. The pandemic revealed with absolute clarity that the communities most devastated by educational disruption were the communities that Catholic schools were originally founded to serve: rural children without digital infrastructure, tribal children in areas with no connectivity, girls pulled out of school permanently during lockdown in communities where female education is already structurally devalued.

Female literacy in Bengal lags behind male literacy by more than two decades of accumulated neglect. The adivasi girl in Purulia who never reaches secondary school. The girl in the tea garden community of North Bengal who is told she cannot cope with English medium. The girl whose parents calculate that her marriage is more valuable than her education. *These are the students for whom Jesus built the school.*

They are waiting. They have always been waiting.

The question that ends Part III of our series — the same question that Moses put to Pharaoh, the same question that Matthew 25 will put to every institution on the last day — is this:

*For whom is this here?*

The gate of Christian education was built facing the poor. The re-visioning is not a new idea. It is a return to the founding face.

Until and unless our institutions measure their success by what happens to the child without the fee receipt — rather than by what happens to the child who already had everything — the word "mission" on our school crest remains a description of what we once were, not of what we are.

And that gap — between what we profess and what we practice — is the theological problem that this chapter, and this series, refuses to let us escape.

## 🕯 CLOSING PRAYER (3 minutes)

*Lord Jesus —
Teacher who sat not only in the synagogues and on the hillsides
but on the ground,
at the level of those who had no seat of honor —

You said: I have come that they may have life,
life in all its fullness.
Not life for the already-full.
Not abundance for those who already have enough.
*Zoe perisson* — overflowing life —
for the ones who are told
they do not qualify,
they cannot cope,
they do not fit the culture of the institution.

We confess that we have built institutions in your name
that have, slowly and with genuinely good intentions,
turned their faces away from the child
you placed at the center.

We confess the pseudo-satisfaction —
the charity galas,
the scholarship photographs in the annual report,
the language of mission
covering a practice of market.

Give us the honesty to look at what we have built
and see it as it is,
not as it was intended.

Give us the courage to do
what re-visioning requires:
not cosmetic changes,
not new programmes
layered over unchanged priorities,
but structural transformation —
the kind that costs something real.

Show us the face of the child at the gate.
The secondhand uniform.
The absent fee receipt.
The Bengali accent that marks difference.
The breakfast not yet eaten.

And let us see in that face
what you see:
your image.
Your presence.
Yourself.

*Gravissimum Educationis* calls education
"a genuine right of every person."
Let our institutions
make that right real.

Not eventually.
Now.

Amen.*
