## OPENING: THE PROBLEMATIC OF CEREMONIAL EQUALITY
*(7 minutes)*

By way of introducing this fourteenth reflection in our series, let me begin with a theological affirmation that commands virtually universal assent — and then with a reality that contradicts it in ways so structurally embedded that they have become largely invisible.

We say: woman and man are equally made in the image of God. *Genesis 1:27* is unambiguous — *"male and female he created them"* — and the image of God is not distributed between the sexes in unequal portions. The theological anthropology of the creation narrative places no qualification on the *imago Dei* as it applies to women. Paul, in *Galatians 3:28*, draws the full New Covenant consequence: *"There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is neither male nor female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus."* And the Second Vatican Council in *Gaudium et Spes* 29 states explicitly that *"every type of discrimination, whether social or cultural, whether based on sex, race, color, social condition, language, or religion, is to be overcome and eradicated as contrary to God's intent."*

That is the claim.

And now the reality.

Every year, on March 8, religious communities across West Bengal and India observe International Women's Day. Masses are offered for women. Special blessings are given. Women's groups do the readings. Roses are distributed. Warm words are spoken about the irreplaceable dignity and contribution of women to the family, the Church, and the society. And on March 9 — the structural conditions that occasioned the celebration are, in the overwhelming majority of institutions, entirely unchanged. The same decision-making rooms have the same demographics. The same formation programmes carry the same unexamined assumptions about the proper sphere of women's authority. The same community cultures practice the same subordinations, sometimes gently, sometimes not.

The consequences of this gap — between the annual celebration and the structural reality — are not trivial.

First: the Church's moral credibility on questions of women's dignity in the broader society is compromised by the gap between its doctrinal affirmation and its institutional practice. A Church that proclaims the equal *imago Dei* of women while maintaining structures that functionally subordinate women cannot speak with prophetic authority on the full range of women's rights questions — trafficking, domestic violence, wage equality, political representation — without the world hearing the structural contradiction beneath the proclamation.

Second: the women within our communities and institutions absorb the message of the structural reality as surely as they hear the message of the annual celebration. And the structural message is frequently: your dignity is affirmed in principle and limited in practice. You are celebrated one day per year and managed the other three hundred and sixty-four.

Third: and this is the most theologically searching consequence — the woman who has internalized the structural subordination of her community, and who now perpetuates it toward women under her own authority, is the evidence that oppression does not require an external oppressor. Once it has been sufficiently normalized, it reproduces itself from within. Sr Inigo names this reality directly: women have sometimes aided in perpetuating the very structures that oppress them — not from malice but from the comprehensiveness of a formation that presented subordination as natural order.

In one sentence, if I were to state the thesis of this reflection: **The Church's celebration of women's dignity will remain ceremonial rather than structural until it is grounded in the full theological weight of the Magnificat — Mary's declaration of the overthrow of every arrangement that diminishes the human person — and until that theological grounding produces specific, institutional, non-cosmetic change in the communities that sing it every evening at Vespers.**

Let me develop this across four movements.

## PART I — TWO MODELS OF WOMEN'S DIGNITY: CEREMONIAL AND STRUCTURAL
*(10 minutes)*

There are two operative models of women's dignity in the contemporary Church — and indeed in contemporary society — that produce radically different institutional and pastoral realities. The difference between them is not the sincerity with which dignity is affirmed. Both models affirm women's dignity. The difference is the depth at which the affirmation is allowed to operate.

I shall call them the *ceremonial model* and the *structural model*.

**The ceremonial model** understands the affirmation of women's dignity as a matter of recognition, celebration, and rhetorical emphasis. In this model, the Church honours women by naming their contributions, venerating their exemplars — particularly Mary and the women saints — conducting special observances, and ensuring that language about women is respectful and appreciative. The ceremonial model is not insincere. It is genuinely convinced of what it affirms. But it locates the affirmation at the level of sentiment and symbol rather than at the level of structure and power.

The specific consequence of the ceremonial model is what I would call *the dignity gap*: the distance between the elevation of women in symbol — Mary as Queen of Heaven, woman as the heart of the family and the Church, the irreplaceable gifts of the feminine genius — and the exclusion of women from the structural processes by which the Church governs itself, makes its decisions, allocates its resources, and shapes its institutional culture. The ceremonial model can sustain an extraordinarily high rhetoric of women's dignity while presiding over an institutional reality in which women have no voice in the rooms where consequential decisions are made.

And the March 8 rose distribution is its liturgical expression: a genuine gesture of appreciation that, by its very ceremonial containment, releases the remaining three hundred and sixty-four days from the obligation of structural change.

Pope Saint John Paul II — who developed an extensive theology of the *genius femininus* and who wrote a deeply appreciative apostolic letter *Mulieris Dignitatem* on the dignity and vocation of women — was nonetheless capable of the honest admission, in his 1995 Letter to Women, that *"women's dignity has often been unacknowledged, undervalued, and ignored."* The admission is significant precisely because it comes from the author of an eloquent theology of women's dignity. The gap between the eloquence and the institutional reality was something he felt compelled to name.

**The structural model** takes the theological affirmation of women's dignity seriously enough to allow it to interrogate the institutional arrangements of the communities that profess it. In this model, the question is not merely: do we celebrate women? The question is: do the structures of our community — its decision-making processes, its allocation of authority and responsibility, its formation practices, its culture of relationship — actually embody the equal *imago Dei* that we affirm in principle?

The structural model does not require that all institutional differentiation be abolished. It requires that every institutional arrangement be evaluated against the theological criterion of the equal dignity of the human person — and that arrangements which fail this evaluation be named as failures and changed, rather than justified by appeal to tradition, natural law, or the complementarity of roles.

Sr Inigo's framework from the National Conference of Religious India — the paradigm shift from *"inward-looking women withdrawn from the world"* to *"women with a global vision for prophetic mission,"* and from *"appendix-daughter of a colonial mother"* to *"consecrated woman of and for her Motherland"* — is a structural programme, not a ceremonial one. It calls for the reorganization of the institutional self-understanding, the mission priorities, and the formation culture of religious women in India. It is asking: what does it mean for a religious woman to be genuinely Indian, genuinely rooted, genuinely prophetic — rather than a faithful replication of a European template that was never designed for this soil?

This is the shift from the ceremonial to the structural model. And it is a shift that the Magnificat demands.

## PART II — ETYMOLOGICAL RECOVERY: WHAT *MEGALYNEI* AND *TAPEINŌSIS* ACTUALLY CLAIM
*(8 minutes)*

Let me now perform the etymological recovery that this argument requires — returning the Magnificat to its original verbal and social force, before centuries of liturgical domestication converted it from a social manifesto into a devotional text.

The Magnificat opens with *"My soul magnifies the Lord"* — *megalynei* in Greek, from *megas* (great) and the verbal suffix *lynō* (to make, to cause to be). The soul of Mary *makes great* the Lord — it amplifies, it enlarges, it gives visible expression to a reality that might otherwise remain invisible. The Magnificat is not a private act of personal gratitude. It is a public declaration — an enlargement of the divine reality into the social and political space.

And the social content of that enlargement is named with precision in *Luke 1:51-53*: *"He has shown strength with his arm; he has scattered the proud in the thoughts of their hearts. He has brought down the powerful from their thrones, and lifted up the lowly; he has filled the hungry with good things, and sent the rich away empty."* These are not metaphors for spiritual states. They are social realities — the redistribution of power, the reversal of the arrangements that concentrate privilege and deprivation along the same social fault-lines as every unjust society.

But the word I want to recover is *tapeinōsis* — usually translated as "lowly estate" or "humble condition" — in *Luke 1:48*: *"He has looked with favour on the tapeinōsis of his servant."* The Greek *tapeinōsis* means, literally, *a state of humiliation* — not the spiritual virtue of humility, but the social condition of one who has been pressed down, subordinated, made small by the operation of social arrangements that assign lower status by virtue of birth, gender, or class.

Mary is not celebrating her spiritual humility. She is naming her social condition — the condition of a young woman in first-century Palestine, with no legal standing, no political voice, no economic independence, and a pregnancy that placed her at the mercy of her betrothed and her community. And she is declaring that God has seen that condition — has looked with favour on the *tapeinōsis* — and has chosen, from within it, to inaugurate the overthrow of every social arrangement that produces it.

The Magnificat is sung every evening at Vespers in communities across the world. It has been so thoroughly absorbed into the liturgical texture of the Church that its social radicalism has been largely neutralized. We hear the words. We do not hear what they are saying.

What they are saying is this: the God who looked upon Mary's *tapeinōsis* sees the *tapeinōsis* of every woman who has been pressed down, subordinated, made small — by the operation of social arrangements in her family, her community, her Church. And the divine response to that *tapeinōsis* is not spiritual consolation. It is structural reversal.

Until the communities that sing the Magnificat allow its *tapeinōsis* logic to interrogate their own institutional arrangements — to ask *"where are women being pressed down by the operation of structures we maintain?"* — they are singing a revolutionary text as a devotional exercise. They have converted a verb into a noun: *megalynei*, the active making-great of God's justice, into *Magnificat*, a liturgical category harmlessly contained within Vespers.

## PART III — THE SCRIPTURAL CONSTELLATION
*(9 minutes)*

I want to bring four scriptural texts into this argument — each adding a dimension the others do not supply.

**The first witness: Luke 1:46-55 — the Magnificat entire.**

The Magnificat must be read whole and read politically before it can be read devotionally. Mary is not a passive recipient of divine favour who expresses her gratitude in elevated poetry. She is a young woman in a politically occupied, economically stratified, gender-hierarchical society who has received, in the Annunciation, a vocation of world-historical consequence — and who responds to that vocation with a declaration of what God's entry into history means for every social arrangement that diminishes the human person.

The structure of the Magnificat is significant: it moves from the personal (*"my soul magnifies"*) to the cosmic (*"he has scattered the proud"*) to the political (*"he has brought down the powerful"*) to the economic (*"he has filled the hungry"*). This is not an accidental sequence. It is a theological argument: God's action in Mary is the particular instance of God's universal pattern of intervention on behalf of those in *tapeinōsis* — and that pattern has consequences for every level of human social organization.

Walter Brueggemann notes that the prophetic imagination always contains both a *critical* and an *energizing* function: it names what is wrong in the present arrangement, and it opens a vision of what God intends instead. The Magnificat does both simultaneously. It names the arrangements that keep the humble down and the powerful enthroned. And it announces their overthrow — not as a future aspiration but as a present reality already inaugurated in the divine choice of Mary.

**The second witness: Galatians 3:28.**

*"There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is neither male nor female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus."*

Paul is not making a statement about the irrelevance of difference. He is making a statement about the irrelevance of *hierarchy* — the ordering of persons into superiors and inferiors on the basis of birth, class, or gender. The distinctions remain. The hierarchy is abolished in Christ.

The history of the interpretation of this text within the institutional Church has been a history of progressive narrowing: the abolition of Jewish-Gentile hierarchy was accepted; the abolition of slave-free hierarchy took eighteen centuries of moral struggle; the abolition of male-female hierarchy remains, in many of its institutional expressions, incomplete. The interpretive principle that allowed the earlier hierarchies to be abolished — the equal dignity of persons before God — is the same principle that the remaining hierarchy cannot ultimately withstand.

**The third witness: Luke 8:1-3.**

*"The twelve were with him, as well as some women who had been cured of evil spirits and infirmities: Mary, called Magdalene... and Joanna... and Susanna, and many others, who provided for them out of their resources."*

This text is almost never preached as a text about women's ministry. It is mentioned in passing as background to the preaching tours of Jesus. But its structural significance is considerable: the women are named, they are travelling with Jesus and the Twelve as a named part of the movement, and they are funding it — *"out of their resources."* The mission of Jesus was financially sustained by women. The movement that became the Church was literally resourced by women whose names Luke carefully preserves.

And Mary Magdalene — named first among the women here, and given by John's Gospel the first resurrection appearance and the commission to proclaim the resurrection to the Eleven — is, in the most literal sense, the apostle to the apostles. The resurrection of Jesus, the central event of Christian faith, was first communicated by a woman, to men who did not believe her (*Luke 24:11*: *"these words seemed to them an idle tale, and they did not believe them"*).

The institutional consequences of this pattern in Jesus's own practice — women named, resourced, commissioned — have been drawn with remarkable inconsistency across the tradition.

**The fourth witness: John 4:7-26 — the Samaritan woman.**

Jesus's conversation with the Samaritan woman at the well is his longest recorded conversation in the Fourth Gospel. His interlocutor is a woman, a Samaritan (twice marginalized), and someone whose personal history is publicly known and would have placed her outside the circle of respectable religious discourse. And Jesus engages her — not as a pastoral case, not as an object of mercy — as a theological interlocutor. It is to her, before anyone else in the Gospel of John, that he reveals himself explicitly as the Messiah: *"I am he, the one who is speaking to you"* (*John 4:26*).

The person who receives the most explicit Christological self-disclosure in the Fourth Gospel, before the passion narrative, is a Samaritan woman of compromised social standing. This is not accidental. It is the Magnificat logic operating in the specific texture of Jesus's ministry: God looks with favour on the *tapeinōsis* — and speaks, to precisely those the dominant social order has made most small, the most profound words.

## PART IV — THE HISTORICAL DIAGNOSIS AND THE INDIAN PARADIGM SHIFT
*(8 minutes)*

The historical diagnosis I want to offer is this: the gap between the Church's theological affirmation of women's equal dignity and its institutional practice is not the product of individual failure or malice. It is the product of a specific, historically locatable theological synthesis that imported into Christian anthropology the hierarchical gender assumptions of Graeco-Roman social philosophy — and then generated theological justifications for those assumptions that had the effect of subordinating a theological datum (the equal *imago Dei*) to a cultural inheritance (the natural inferiority of women).

Aristotle, in the *Politics* and the *Generation of Animals*, held that women were, by nature, incomplete men — defective in reason, emotion, and social capacity. This was not merely a philosophical opinion. It was the dominant biological and political framework of the Graeco-Roman world into which early Christianity was born. And the Fathers of the Church — who were formed intellectually in that world, who absorbed its categories before they converted to Christianity — did not uniformly subject those categories to the radical critique that the theological datum of the equal *imago Dei* required.

The consequence is a tradition that contains, in permanent tension, the Magnificat and Aristotle's defective-male thesis — the radical theological affirmation of women's dignity and the cultural subordination of women's authority — and that has, across most of its history, managed this tension by allowing the cultural inheritance to govern institutional practice while the theological datum was assigned to the domain of spiritual aspiration.

The International Women's Day history that Sr Inigo narrates is the secular expression of the same tension and the same demand for resolution: fifteen thousand women marching in New York in 1908 were demanding, in the political register, what the Magnificat had declared in the theological register more than nineteen hundred years earlier. The world was not done with Mary's announcement. The *tapeinōsis* was not yet reversed. The *megalynei* was still needed.

Now, the Indian consequence of this historical diagnosis must be stated in two registers.

**First: the colonial inheritance.** The phrase *"appendix-daughter of a colonial mother"* — introduced in the NCRI framework that Sr Inigo cites — names a specific form of the ceremonial-structural gap as it operates in Indian religious congregations with European founding origins. These congregations were established with genuine charity, genuine sacrifice, and genuine faith. Their founders came and served at real personal cost. This is not in question.

What is in question is the institutional form in which the charism was transmitted. The constitutional documents, the spiritual culture, the aesthetic preferences, the mission priorities, the formation assumptions — all were designed for a European social context and imposed on an Indian one. And the consequence, for Indian women religious in particular, has been a form of consecrated life in which genuine Indian spiritual resources — the rich contemplative traditions of this subcontinent, the prophetic wisdom of its marginalized communities, the ecological attentiveness of its adivasi spiritual heritage, the theological creativity of its Dalit Christian thinkers — were systematically marginalised in favour of a European template that was never designed to hold them.

To become a *"consecrated woman of and for her Motherland"* is not to abandon the founding charism. It is to inhabit it fully enough to incarnate it appropriately — as the Incarnation itself incarnated the divine Word in a specific culture, language, and social context rather than in a transcultural abstraction.

**Second: the Lenten convergence.** Sr Inigo observes that International Women's Day — March 8 — falls invariably within the Season of Lent. This convergence is, she argues, providential. Lent is the season of honest self-confrontation, of naming the gaps between what is professed and what is practiced, of identifying the places where conversion is structurally required rather than merely spiritually desired. The Lenten examination of conscience, as it has been traditionally practiced, asks: where have I failed in prayer, fasting, and almsgiving? It rarely asks: where has my community failed in its structural treatment of women?

The convergence of Lent and Women's Day is an annual invitation — which most communities have not yet accepted — to widen the Lenten examination of conscience to include the structural question: does the life of this community embody the equal *imago Dei* that *Genesis 1:27* and *Galatians 3:28* declare? Does it honour, in its decision-making culture, its formation practices, and its allocation of authority, the woman who funded the mission of Jesus and received the first resurrection commission?

If the answer is no — and in most communities, the honest answer is a qualified no — then Lent is the season in which that answer can be faced, named, and responded to. Not with guilt, which is unproductive. With the metanoia that Lent is designed to produce: a turning, structural and personal, in the direction of what the Gospel actually requires.

## PROPHETIC DEMAND & SILENCE
*(3 minutes)*

Let me close with a demand — and then with the silence the demand requires.

Mary sang the Magnificat. She was a teenager, unmarried, in a culture where her pregnancy could have resulted in social destruction. From that position of maximum *tapeinōsis*, she opened her mouth and declared the overthrow of every social arrangement that diminishes the human person. And she declared it in the present tense: *"He has scattered. He has brought down. He has filled. He has sent away."* Not: he will do these things in the eschatological future. He has done them. The inauguration is complete. The structure of the new world has been established in the divine act of entering human history in a woman's womb.

The communities that sing this text at Vespers every evening are singing a present-tense declaration of structural reversal. They are, in the most literal sense, committing themselves every evening — by the act of singing it — to the social programme it describes. The question is whether the daily singing of the Magnificat is allowed to interrogate the daily practice of the community that sings it.

Until and unless the communities that sing the Magnificat allow *tapeinōsis* — in whatever form it operates within their institutional life — to be named and addressed structurally rather than celebrated ceremonially, the Magnificat will continue to be the most revolutionary text in the New Testament sung as the most domesticated devotion in the liturgy.

The question I leave with you is one:

**In your community's institutional life — its decision-making processes, its formation culture, its allocation of authority — what is the specific structural arrangement that your nightly singing of the Magnificat has not yet been allowed to interrogate? And what would it cost your community to allow it?**

*(Sustained silence — minimum four minutes. Do not fill it.)*

## CLOSING PRAYER

*Mary —
you sang it before you understood
what it would cost you to mean it.
You sang it as a teenager
from the position of maximum *tapeinōsis*:
unmarried, pregnant, politically occupied,
in a world where your testimony
would not have been accepted in court.

And from that position
you declared the overthrow
of every arrangement that pressed you down.
Not as future hope.
As present reality.

We have sung your song every evening for centuries.
We have venerated your image in every chapel.
We have named our schools and hospitals
and congregations after you.

And we confess:
the world your Magnificat described —
where the humble are lifted,
where the hungry are filled,

that world is still being waited for
by the women in our congregations,
our parishes, our formation houses,
our decision-making rooms.

Forgive us for the ceremonial equality
that releases the structural inequality
from the obligation of change.

that has not yet been allowed
to reorganize the morning meeting.

to match the theological conviction.

## SPEAKER NOTES

**Register:** Academic formation talk — the register of Fr. Joseph Lobo SJ. This talk engages a subject on which the audience may hold a range of views, some of them resistant. The Lobo method of grounding the argument in the Church's own theological tradition — *Genesis 1:27*, *Galatians 3:28*, the Magnificat, *Gaudium et Spes* 29 — is essential here. The speaker is not importing an external feminist programme. They are holding the Church accountable to its own most foundational texts.

**Key theological moves:**

1. The **ceremonial vs. structural model** of women's dignity — with the ceremonial model traced to its specific institutional consequence: the dignity gap between the elevation of women in symbol and their exclusion from the structures of power and decision-making. The March 8 rose distribution is its liturgical expression — named with warmth and irony, not contempt.

2. ***Megalynei*** and ***tapeinōsis*** recovered etymologically: *megalynei* from a devotional text back to its active verbal force (making-great, amplifying the divine into social and political space); *tapeinōsis* from a spiritual virtue (humility) back to a social condition (structural subordination, being pressed down). Together, they establish that the Magnificat is a political declaration of structural reversal — not a private act of piety.

3. The **scriptural constellation**: Luke 1:46-55 (the Magnificat entire — read whole and read politically, with Brueggemann's criticizing/energizing structure), Galatians 3:28 (the abolition of hierarchy, not difference — with its unfinished interpretive history), Luke 8:1-3 (the named women who funded the mission of Jesus), John 4:7-26 (the Samaritan woman as primary recipient of the Johannine Christological self-disclosure — the Magnificat logic in the texture of Jesus's ministry).

4. The **historical diagnosis**: the importation of Aristotle's defective-male thesis into Christian anthropology via the Fathers — creating the permanent tension between the *imago Dei* datum and the cultural subordination practice — with the March 8 movement as the secular expression of the same demand for resolution that the Magnificat had already made in the theological register.

5. The **two-register Indian consequence**: the colonial inheritance (appendix-daughter condition — named with pastoral care as an invitation to self-examination, not a condemnation of founding congregations) and the Lenten convergence (the annual invitation to widen the examination of conscience to include the structural treatment of women — an invitation most communities have not accepted).

**Secular and theological thinkers deployed:**
- Aristotle's *Politics* and *Generation of Animals*: the philosophical genealogy of the subordination (named precisely so the argument has a historical location, not a timeless inevitability)
- Walter Brueggemann: criticizing and energizing functions of prophetic imagination (applied to the Magnificat's dual structure)
- The 1908 New York march and its NCRI framework: secular and ecclesial expressions of the same demand for structural resolution of the ceremonial-structural gap

**Church documents cited:**
- *Genesis 1:27*: equal *imago Dei* of male and female
- *Galatians 3:28*: abolition of hierarchy in Christ
- *Gaudium et Spes* 29: discrimination based on sex contrary to God's intent
- Pope Saint John Paul II, Letter to Women (1995): honest admission of ecclesial failure
- Pope Saint John Paul II, *Mulieris Dignitatem*: theology of *genius femininus* — cited precisely because the gap between its eloquence and institutional practice is itself evidence of the ceremonial-structural problem

**Pacing:**
- The Opening must establish the three consequences of the ceremonial-structural gap with precision before the thesis. The gap between annual celebration and unchanged structures must be named without being harsh — it is a diagnosis, not an accusation.
- Part I (Two Models) — the ceremonial model must be treated with genuine respect for its sincerity before its inadequacy is named. The rose distribution is not contemptible. It is insufficient.
- Part II (Etymological Recovery) — *tapeinōsis* is the key. The recovery of Mary's social condition as the ground of the Magnificat's political content is the move that reframes everything. Take time with it.
- Part III (Scriptural Constellation) — the Samaritan woman receives the Johannine Christological self-disclosure before Peter. This is not peripheral. Name it with the weight it deserves.
- Part IV (Historical Diagnosis) — the Aristotle-to-Fathers genealogy should be stated precisely and briefly: the point is not to condemn the Fathers but to locate the distortion historically, giving the community permission to name it as historically contingent rather than theologically necessary.
- The Lenten convergence paragraph is the creative heart of the chapter's contribution. Slow down here.
- The silence after the closing demand is non-negotiable. Four minutes minimum.
