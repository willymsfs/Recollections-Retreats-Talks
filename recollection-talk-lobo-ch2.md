## OPENING: THE PROBLEMATIC OF IDENTITY
*(7 minutes)*

By way of introducing this second reflection in our series, let me begin with a well-known theological affirmation — and then with the reality that contradicts it in specific and observable ways.

We say: every consecrated person possesses a distinct identity, rooted in a charism, expressed in a specific spirituality, and directed toward a concrete mission. The Second Vatican Council in *Perfectae Caritatis* 2 affirms that the renewal of consecrated life depends upon a return to the founding sources of each institute — to the spirit of the founders — and upon an adaptation of the institute to the changed conditions of the times.

That is the claim.

And now the reality.

Ask most religious today — priests, sisters, brothers — to describe their identity, and they will tell you: the name of their congregation, the number of their institutions, the year of their founding, and perhaps the habit they wear. They will tell you, in other words, about their *coordinates* — where they are located on the ecclesiastical map — but not about their *substance* — what flows from them and gives life. The difference between coordinates and substance is precisely the difference between a labeled bottle and what is inside it.

The consequences of this confusion are not trivial.

First: when identity is reduced to coordinates, the renewal of consecrated life becomes a project of institutional management rather than charismatic re-founding. You polish the coordinates. You update the logo. You revise the website. But the substance — the founding fire that gave everything else its meaning — remains unexamined and increasingly depleted.

Second: when identity is mislocated in external markers — habit, campus, statistical strength, bank balance — the congregation becomes unable to critique these markers even when they work against the mission. The institution protects itself from the charism that gave it birth.

Third: and this is the most serious consequence for the formation context — when young men and women in initial formation absorb a coordinates-based understanding of religious identity, they are being formed for institutional belonging, not for prophetic discipleship. They learn to identify with the congregation; they do not learn to be interrogated by it.

In one sentence, if I were to state the thesis of this reflection: **Religious identity is a theological category, not a sociological one — and until consecrated persons recover identity as a dynamic, charismatic, and prophetically demanding reality rather than a static institutional status, the renewal of consecrated life will remain at the level of renovation rather than re-founding.**

I shall now develop that thesis in four movements.

## PART I — TWO MODELS OF RELIGIOUS IDENTITY
*(10 minutes)*

Let me identify two models of religious identity that are, in practice, operative in our congregations today. I shall call them, for the sake of precision, the *parametric model* and the *charismatic model*.

**The parametric model** — which is, I would argue, the default model in most of our congregations — defines identity by measurable parameters: numerical strength, institutional expansion, geographical spread, architectural visibility, financial security. Sr Inigo SSA, in the chapter that grounds this reflection, names these parameters with surgical honesty: distinctive dress, the number of convents opened, the size of campuses, the accumulation of financial assets, and what she calls the *"frenetic rush of concrete results."*

Now, I want to be careful here. These parameters are not intrinsically wrong. A congregation that grows in membership, that builds institutions for the poor, that develops financial stability in order to sustain mission — none of this is theologically suspect in itself. The problem is not the parameters. The problem is when the parameters become the *definition* of identity rather than the *expression* of it.

And the specific consequence of the parametric model is what I would call *identity anxiety* — a chronic institutional preoccupation with measuring, comparing, and defending one's position on the ecclesiastical map. A congregation living primarily in the parametric model is always looking over its shoulder at other congregations. It measures its significance by comparative growth. It interprets decline in membership as a theological crisis rather than as, possibly, a pneumatological purification. And it becomes incapable of the specific freedom that genuine mission requires — the freedom to let go of an institution when the institution has ceased to serve the charism.

**The charismatic model** — which *Perfectae Caritatis* 2 points toward, even if it does not name it in these terms — defines identity dynamically. Identity in this model is not a possession but a direction. It is not what a congregation has acquired but where it is consistently oriented. It flows from a founding encounter with God — an encounter that was, as Sr Inigo observes, genuinely disruptive for the founder — through a specific spirituality, toward a concrete mission, and expresses itself in ministries that serve the mission rather than substituting for it.

The distinction between these two models produces, in practice, a series of concrete differences.

In the parametric model, the question asked about any new ministry is: *"Will this enhance our visibility and strengthen our institutional position?"* In the charismatic model, the question is: *"Does this flow from our charism, and does it take us toward those whom our charism sent us to serve?"*

In the parametric model, the departure of members from a congregation is experienced primarily as institutional loss. In the charismatic model, it is experienced as an occasion for communal discernment: *"What is the Spirit saying to us through this movement?"*

In the parametric model, formation produces institutional loyalists. In the charismatic model, formation produces — or is designed to produce — prophetic disciples.

I want to come to the question of formation in greater detail a little later. But first let me turn to the central theological concept of this chapter: the meaning of *charism* itself.

## PART II — ETYMOLOGICAL RECOVERY: WHAT "CHARISM" ACTUALLY MEANS
*(8 minutes)*

The word *charism* — *charisma* in Greek — is derived from *charis*, meaning grace or gift. But the specificity of *charisma* over against *charis* is important and is almost always lost in translation.

*Charis* is grace in its general sense: the gracious favor of God toward creation and humanity. *Charisma* — the word Paul uses in *1 Corinthians 12* — is a *specific, particular, directed* gift: a grace that has a shape, a target, and a purpose beyond the recipient. Paul's entire argument in *1 Corinthians 12:4-11* is that charisms are diverse precisely because the needs they address are diverse, and that their diversity is a sign of the Spirit's wisdom, not a problem to be managed into uniformity.

Now, over the centuries, and particularly in the post-Tridentine period, *charisma* underwent the same reversal I identified in our first reflection with respect to *consecrare*: it was converted from a **verb** into a **noun**. From a living movement of the Spirit, directed outward toward specific human need, it became a static possession: something a congregation *has* rather than something a congregation *does* and *becomes*.

When charism becomes a noun, several things follow. First: it becomes something to be preserved rather than exercised. The congregation develops a *charism-preservation apparatus* — constitutions, traditions, ceremonies, formation curricula — all designed to protect what is, in its original nature, a living fire. And the specific irony is that fire cannot be preserved. It can only be fed or allowed to die.

Second: when charism becomes a noun, it becomes justification for institutional continuity regardless of whether that continuity serves the original movement of the Spirit. Congregations continue doing what they have always done — not because their charism calls them to it in the present context, but because it is what they have always done. The charism, in other words, is invoked to legitimate the status quo rather than to interrogate it.

Third: when charism becomes a noun, the founder is canonized — spiritually and sometimes literally — and their charism is enshrined rather than inhabited. The founder becomes a museum exhibit rather than a living interlocutor. And the community periodically visits the museum — on feast days, at chapter meetings, during anniversaries — without allowing the founding vision to make any actual demands on the present.

The recovery of charism as a verb — as a living, directed, prophetically disruptive movement of the Spirit — is what *Perfectae Caritatis* 2 means when it calls for a return to *"the spirit of the founders."* Not the *form* of the founders. The *spirit*. The founding encounter with God that produced such urgency, such creativity, such willingness to bear social and ecclesiastical cost, that an institution eventually grew around it.

Sr Inigo's description of the founders — people who were *"exceedingly disturbed and obsessed"* until they responded adequately to their charism — is not hagiographical sentiment. It is a precise theological statement. The disturbance is the mark of an authentic charism at work. When the disturbance ceases — when the congregation becomes comfortable — the question must be asked: has the charism been fulfilled, or has it been suppressed?

In most cases, it has not been fulfilled. It has been suppressed by institutional comfort.

## PART III — THE SCRIPTURAL CONSTELLATION
*(9 minutes)*

I want to bring three texts into conversation — each adding a dimension that the others do not supply.

**The first witness: 1 Corinthians 12:4-7.**

*"Now there are varieties of gifts, but the same Spirit; and there are varieties of service, but the same Lord; and there are varieties of activities, but it is the same God who activates all of them in everyone. To each is given the manifestation of the Spirit for the common good."*

The phrase I want to draw attention to is *"for the common good"* — in Greek, *pros to sympheron*, which means, literally, *toward that which brings together and benefits*. A charism is not given for the enhancement of the individual recipient, nor for the institutional enrichment of the congregation that receives it. It is given *toward* a convergence — a bringing-together of what is scattered, a healing of what is broken, a nourishing of what is depleted.

This means — and the consequence must be stated plainly — that a charism exercised primarily for the congregation's own institutional benefit has been diverted from its theological purpose. It has been taken out of circulation. And when the currency of grace is hoarded rather than circulated, it does to the congregation what hoarded currency does to an economy: it generates internal inflation and external poverty simultaneously.

**The second witness: John 1:12-13.**

*"To all who received Him, who believed in His name, He gave the right to become children of God — who were born, not of blood or of the will of the flesh or of the will of man, but of God."*

The anchor text of this series. But I want to read it now against the background of everything we have said about identity.

Note the threefold negation: *not of blood, not of the will of the flesh, not of the will of man.* John is dismantling three alternative accounts of identity. Identity is not genealogical — it does not come from which family, which caste, which ethnic community one belongs to. Identity is not biological — it does not emerge from natural capacity or human achievement. And identity is not institutional — it does not flow from the will of any human structure, however sacred.

Identity — according to John — is *theogenic*: born of God. It is entirely from outside. It cannot be manufactured, maintained, or managed. It can only be received. And the one who receives it is given, not a title, but a *right* — an *exousia*, which is the same word John uses elsewhere to describe the authority of Jesus himself. *Exousia* in John is always authority that flows from one's deepest identity, not authority that is imposed from without.

The consecrated person's identity, in this reading, is not their habit, their congregation, their ministerial title, or their institutional position. It is their status as *child of God* — a status that precedes every institutional affiliation and survives every institutional crisis. And the practical consequence is this: a religious whose primary identity is charismatic rather than parametric cannot be destabilised by institutional decline, because their identity does not reside in the institution.

**The third witness: James 2:18.**

*"Show me your faith without works, and I will show you my faith by what I do."*

James is not making a Protestant-Catholic argument about faith and works. He is making a deeper epistemological claim: that identity is only ever known through its expressions. The interior reality — faith, charism, spiritual commitment — is real, but it is not self-evidencing. It must be performed. Not performed in the theatrical sense, but in the philosophical sense: it must be *enacted* in the public world, in concrete acts that are recognizable to others as expressions of a particular life.

This is what it means to say that charism must produce a recognizable identity in the community being served. The people of Purulia, the slum-dwellers of North Kolkata, the Santali villagers of Birbhum — they do not read constitutions. They do not attend chapter meetings. They experience congregational identity through the specific quality of presence and service they receive. And if that quality is indistinguishable from what a competent secular NGO provides, then the charism — however well-preserved in institutional form — has ceased to produce its proper fruit.

Cardinal Emmanuel Suhard once said that the Christian — and the same applies with greater intensity to the consecrated person — must *"live in such a way that one's whole life would be inexplicable if God did not exist."* That is the test of charismatic identity. Not: is the congregation growing? But: is this life theologically inexplicable by any other means than divine charism?

## PART IV — THE HISTORICAL DIAGNOSIS AND ITS INDIAN CONSEQUENCE
*(8 minutes)*

Let me now attempt the historical diagnosis. When did the reversal from the charismatic to the parametric model occur, and what drove it?

I would locate the decisive shift in the post-Tridentine period — specifically in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries — when the Counter-Reformation Church, responding to the Protestant critique, invested heavily in visible institutional presence as a marker of Catholic identity. Schools, hospitals, missions, confraternities — these were not merely charitable works. They were, in the post-Tridentine logic, *signs of Catholic vitality* against Protestant challenge. The congregation that built the most, educated the most, converted the most, was demonstrating the superiority of the Catholic form of Christian life.

The consequence — unintended but structurally inevitable — was that institutional productivity became the primary metric of congregational success. And once a metric is established, it generates its own institutional logic. Congregations that were founded in the fifteenth century to serve the destitute in streets and gutters found themselves, by the eighteenth century, running prestigious academies for the bourgeoisie — because that is where the institutional growth was.

Edward Schillebeeckx, in his careful historical analysis of ministry in the Church, makes a related observation: the institutionalization of any charism follows a predictable pattern. The founding urgency produces structures to sustain it. The structures develop their own bureaucratic logic. The bureaucratic logic gradually displaces the founding urgency, not through malice but through the structural tendency of institutions to self-perpetuate. Schillebeeckx calls this the *"hardening"* of charism — and he notes that it is this hardening, not any crisis of faith, that has historically produced the most serious renewals in the Church's life.

Now, the Indian consequence of this historical diagnosis must be stated directly.

The congregations that came to India in the colonial period — many of them in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries — brought with them this post-Tridentine institutional logic already fully formed. They built schools, hospitals, and training institutions of genuine value. But they built them within a social logic that was also colonial: education in English, for the upwardly mobile, oriented toward the formation of a Westernised Indian Catholic professional class.

The charism — in many cases, a charism genuinely directed toward the poor and marginalised — was gradually re-routed through the prestige institutions toward populations that were not the original intended recipients of the founding vision.

From a sociological point of view, Max Weber is emphatic in noting that religious charisma undergoes *"routinization"* — a conversion of the original prophetic urgency into stable, reproducible institutional forms. What Weber describes sociologically, theology must evaluate normatively: not all routinization is betrayal, but all routinization requires periodic prophetic critique if the charism is to remain alive.

In the Indian context specifically — in West Bengal, in the Northeast, in the Dalit and Tribal communities of this region — this critique is not abstract. It asks: who is actually benefiting from the institution? Who is being reached that would not otherwise be reached? And who is not being reached — not because they are far away, but because the institutional logic has reoriented the congregation toward populations that generate more sustainable support?

Sr Inigo's framework of genuine identity — charism → spirituality → mission → ministries → identity — is, in the Indian context, a structural critique of every congregation that has allowed the sequence to reverse: that is, every congregation that defines its ministries first, defends them institutionally, and then retrospectively constructs a charism-narrative to justify what is essentially an inheritance of historical social location.

The reversal of this sequence — returning from ministries to mission, from mission to spirituality, from spirituality to charism, from charism to the founding encounter with God — is what *Perfectae Caritatis* 2 means by *"return to the roots."* It is not nostalgia. It is not archaism. It is the rigorous theological discipline of asking: what, in this present moment, does our founding charism require of us? And then having the courage to be told.

## PROPHETIC DEMAND & SILENCE
*(3 minutes)*

I want to close with a demand — not a consolation.

Until and unless consecrated persons recover identity as a charismatic category rather than a parametric one — until charism is rehabilitated as a verb rather than a noun, as a living direction rather than a static possession — the renewal of consecrated life in Asia will remain an institutional project rather than a prophetic one. And institutional projects, however well-funded and well-intentioned, cannot do what only a prophetic life can do.

The question I leave with you is this. One question:

**In your congregation's present institutional life — which ministries are expressions of your charism, and which are inheritances of your historical social location? And are you willing to ask that question honestly enough to let it change something?**

Do not answer now. Sit with it in silence.

*(Sustained silence — minimum four minutes. Do not fill it.)*

## CLOSING PRAYER

*God of the founding encounter —
You disturbed our founders until they responded.
You made them restless, sleepless, socially inconvenient,
theologically suspicious to the comfortable
and dangerously hopeful to the poor.

We confess that we have, in many of our works,
preserved the coordinates of their obedience
while losing the direction of it.

We have kept the name and let the substance thin.
We have kept the building and let the fire go out.
We have kept the timetable and lost the urgency.

Give us the honesty to name this.
Not with guilt — guilt is cheap and changes nothing —
but with the theological sobriety of people
who know that charism is not our property to manage
but Your gift to be exercised.

Disturb us again.
Make us exceedingly uncomfortable
until we respond adequately.

And let the people of this region —
the poor of West Bengal,
the tribal communities of Jharkhand and the Northeast,
the Dalit families who have waited long enough
for the Gospel to reach their lives rather than their doorsteps —
let them be the ones who tell us,
by their response to our presence,
whether our charism is alive.

We ask this in the name of the one
who gave us the right to be children of God —
not subjects of an institution,
not managers of a programme,
but children.
Free, loved, sent.

Amen.*
