## OPENING: THE PROBLEMATIC OF MEDIATED COMMUNICATION
*(7 minutes)*

By way of introducing this fifth reflection in our series, let me begin with a theological affirmation that every person in this room would immediately accept — and then with the reality that contradicts it in ways we have not yet adequately examined.

We say: the consecrated person is called to communicate the Gospel — to be, in the language of Pope Francis in *Evangelii Gaudium* 120, a *"permanent missionary."* And Pope Saint John Paul II, engaging the digital age with theological seriousness in his Apostolic Letter *The Rapid Development* (2005), said plainly that the internet is the best forum currently available for evangelization. The mandate of *Matthew 28:19* — *"Go into all the world and proclaim the Good News"* — is directional and unconditional. It does not specify which medium. It does not exempt us from the media that our contemporaries actually inhabit. The world has moved. Its new geography is substantially digital. And if the consecrated person intends to go where people are, rather than wait for people to come where the institution is comfortable, then the digital world is a genuine and serious pastoral frontier.

That is the claim.

And now the reality.

The same digital infrastructure that makes global evangelization possible has produced, in the communities meant to embody it, a specific and ironic pathology. The superior who is connected to the entire diocesan email network — but not available to the novice who knocks on her door with trembling hands. The priest who posts daily Scripture reflections to three thousand followers — but cannot sit with a parishioner for fifteen minutes without checking his notifications. The congregation that has a beautifully curated social media presence communicating care for the poor — while the internal community life is fractured by non-communication, distraction, and the specific loneliness that screens generate in the very people they appear to connect.

We have confused *broadcasting* with *communicating*.
We have confused *posting* with *being present*.
We have confused *digital reach* with *pastoral depth*.

And beneath all three confusions lies a single theological error: the substitution of mediated communication for incarnational communication — the substitution of the platform for the person.

The consequences of this confusion are not trivial. They are visible in the epidemic of loneliness among the most digitally connected generation in human history. They are visible in the unprecedented mental health crisis among young people whose entire social life is screen-mediated. And they are visible — if we are honest — in the diminishing depth of pastoral relationship within our own communities and ministries.

In one sentence, if I were to state the thesis of this reflection: **The theology of communication adequate to the consecrated life is not a theology of platforms and reach but a theology of incarnation — of the Word becoming flesh, dwelling among us, knowing people by name, and touching what needed to be touched — and digital media, however genuinely useful, must be evaluated against this incarnational standard rather than allowed to substitute for it.**

Let me develop this across four movements.

## PART I — TWO THEOLOGIES OF COMMUNICATION: BROADCAST AND INCARNATIONAL
*(10 minutes)*

There are two models of communication operative in the contemporary world — and, I would argue, operative within our religious communities — that produce radically different pastoral relationships and radically different understandings of what mission actually requires.

I shall call them the *broadcast model* and the *incarnational model*.

**The broadcast model** understands communication as the transmission of content from a sender to receivers. Its primary metrics are reach, engagement, and impact — understood quantitatively. In this model, the communicator is above all a producer of content: homilies posted, reflections shared, videos streamed, messages forwarded. The audience is addressed as a collective. Individual relationship is neither possible nor, within the logic of this model, necessary. What matters is the quality of the content and the size of the platform.

The broadcast model is not without genuine value. The capacity to reach thousands of people simultaneously with truthful, well-formed, Gospel-shaped content is a real gift. During the COVID lockdowns — when physical gathering was impossible — congregations and retreat houses that had developed digital presence were able to maintain some continuity of pastoral accompaniment for communities otherwise entirely cut off. This is not negligible. Pope Saint John Paul II's assessment of the internet as the best available forum for evangelization was not naive. It was theologically serious.

But the broadcast model becomes a pathology — and this is the critical diagnostic point — when it substitutes for incarnational presence rather than supplementing it. When the congregation's digital output becomes the primary measure of its pastoral vitality. When the priest counts his followers rather than his relationships. When the religious community's Instagram presence is more carefully curated than its internal community life. When broadcasting replaces the slower, more demanding, less measurable work of actually being with people.

*Vita Consecrata* 38 — the Church's own document on consecrated life — names precisely this risk when it observes that the *"progressive weakening of religious values"* in contemporary culture is driven by materialism, individualism, and the *"excessive stress on efficiency and personal talents."* The broadcast model, when it becomes dominant, turbocharges all three: materialism becomes algorithmic, individualism becomes personal branding, and efficiency becomes a metric of spiritual vitality measured in views.

**The incarnational model** understands communication not as content transmission but as presence. Its primary category is not reach but relationship. Its measure of success is not engagement but encounter — the specific, unrepeatable encounter between this person and that person, in this place, at this moment, in which something is given and received that no platform can produce or replicate.

The incarnational model is, in the most literal theological sense, the model of the Incarnation itself. The *Logos* — the Word through whom all things were made — did not communicate from a position of divine distance. The *Logos* became *sarx*: flesh. Dwelt among us. Learned our language from the inside. Sat at tables where the conversation was uncomfortable. Stopped in the middle of crowds for one person. Touched what had been rendered untouchable. Called the dead by their name.

This is not a model of communication. It is the definition of communication — the standard against which every other model must be evaluated.

Henri Nouwen, in his theology of ministry, defined the ministry of presence with a precision that I find irreplaceable. He said it means: to know people by name — not their username, their name; to eat and drink with them; to listen to their stories and tell your own; and to let them know — with words, handshakes, and what he called *"hugs"* — that you do not simply like them, but truly love them. This definition contains, embedded within it, an entire theology of communication. And it is a definition in which no screen appears.

The question this first movement puts to every consecrated person in this room is not: *"Do you have a social media presence?"* The question is: *"Which model — broadcast or incarnational — is actually governing your pastoral practice? And is the digital infrastructure in your life serving the incarnational model, or replacing it?"*

## PART II — ETYMOLOGICAL RECOVERY: WHAT *LOGOS* ACTUALLY MEANS
*(8 minutes)*

Let me now perform the etymological recovery that this argument requires — returning a word to its original theological force before it was absorbed into a merely intellectual or informational register.

The Greek *Logos* — the opening word of John's prologue, translated as *"Word"* — is one of the most semantically dense terms in the entire theological tradition. It carries, in the Greek philosophical context from which John appropriates it, the sense of *reason*, *order*, *meaning*, *principle of intelligibility*. The *Logos* in Stoic philosophy is the rational principle that holds the universe together and gives it coherence. When John opens his Gospel with *"In the beginning was the Logos"*, he is claiming that the rational principle of the universe — the source of all intelligibility — is not an abstraction. It is personal. And in *John 1:14*, he makes the claim that has no parallel in any religious or philosophical tradition: *"The Logos became flesh and dwelt among us."*

The *Logos became sarx.* The principle of all meaning became a human body. The source of all intelligibility became a person who could be touched, who grew tired, who wept, who was hungry, who — in the most theologically astonishing verse of the entire New Testament — said from the cross: *"My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?"*

Now, what has happened to *Logos* in the contemporary theology of communication — and in practice, in the digital communication strategies of religious institutions — is the conversion I have traced in our previous reflections: from a verb to a noun, from a living relational reality to a manageable category. *Logos* — the Word that became flesh — has become *"content."* The Gospel has become *"messaging."* Proclamation has become *"posting."* And the profound, costly, irreducibly personal act of giving one's life for another's understanding has become a matter of platform management and engagement metrics.

This conversion has a specific modern genealogy. It is the consequence of what the Canadian philosopher Marshall McLuhan named, in the 1960s, the dominance of the *"medium"* over the *"message"* — the tendency of the technologies of communication to reshape not merely how content is transmitted but what content is understood to be. When the primary medium of communication becomes the broadcast platform, the *Logos* is pressured to become content: something that can be produced, packaged, scheduled, and distributed. And a *Logos* that has been converted into content has lost precisely what the Incarnation was designed to give it: embodied, personal, relational presence.

The recovery of *Logos* as *"the Word who became flesh"* — as opposed to *"the message that was transmitted"* — is the etymological foundation of the incarnational model of communication. And its practical implication is this: every act of genuine Gospel communication must, at some level, bear the marks of the Incarnation. It must be embodied. It must be relational. It must be present. It must be costly. And it must be directed, ultimately, not toward a demographic but toward a person — the specific, unrepeatable, infinitely valued person who is in front of us.

## PART III — THE SCRIPTURAL CONSTELLATION
*(9 minutes)*

I want to bring four scriptural texts into this argument — each adding a dimension that the others do not supply, and together constituting a scriptural theology of communication.

**The first witness: John 1:14.**

*"The Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory."*

The verb *"dwelt"* — *eskēnōsen* in Greek — means, literally, *"pitched his tent."* It is the language of the wilderness: the temporary, vulnerable dwelling of the pilgrim community. The *Logos* did not take up permanent residence in a well-appointed institution. The *Logos* pitched a tent — the dwelling of someone who has come to share the conditions of a people on a journey. This is the Incarnation as a communication strategy: not a broadcast from above, but a tent pitched in the midst of the community being addressed.

The implications for incarnational ministry are immediate. The religious who lives at a careful, comfortable distance from the community they serve — who administers from a position of institutional security — has not yet understood the communication model of the Incarnation. The tent must be pitched in the midst of the people. And tents are, by nature, not secure.

**The second witness: Luke 4:40.**

*"As the sun was setting, all those who had any who were sick with various kinds of diseases brought them to him; and he laid his hands on each of them and cured them."*

The detail I want to draw attention to is *"each of them."* Not: he addressed the crowd. Not: he healed the group. *Each of them.* Individually. One at a time. With his hands. The communication of healing — which is what the Gospel ultimately is — required, in Jesus's own practice, the particularizing of attention: this person, this body, this specific suffering, this touch.

The broadcast model can address thousands. The incarnational model attends to one — and then another, and then another — with undivided attention. This is not inefficiency. It is the only model adequate to the infinite value of the individual person before God.

**The third witness: John 11:21, 32-35.**

*"Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died."* Martha's words. And then Mary, falling at his feet, says the same thing: *"Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died."* And the text says: *"When Jesus saw her weeping, and the Jews who came with her also weeping, he was greatly disturbed in spirit and deeply moved."* And then the shortest verse in the New Testament: *"Jesus wept."*

This text is the most concentrated theology of presence in the entire Gospel tradition. The cry of Martha and Mary — *"if you had been here"* — is the cry of everyone who has experienced absence at the moment of need. And Jesus's response is not an explanation, not a theological justification of the divine plan, not a message delivered from a safe distance. He weeps. He is disturbed in spirit. He is *moved* — the Greek *embrimaomai* suggests a deep, visceral agitation, not a polite expression of sympathy.

This is the communication of presence: the willingness to be genuinely affected by the reality of the person before you. And it is a communication that cannot, by its very nature, be mediated through a platform. The screen cannot weep. The algorithm cannot be *embrimaomai*. The notification cannot substitute for a face that shows, without words, that it has been genuinely reached by your suffering.

**The fourth witness: John 17:4.**

*"I glorified you on earth by finishing the work that you gave me to do."*

This is the anchor text of the chapter — Jesus's own account of what his communication achieved. And it is striking: the criterion is not reach. It is not impact. It is not engagement. The criterion is: *I finished the work you gave me to do.* The communication was faithful to its commission. It was directed by God's purpose, not by its own metrics of success.

This verse is the theological standard against which every act of religious communication — every post, every homily, every WhatsApp forward, every pastoral visit, every social media campaign — must ultimately be evaluated. Not: how many people saw it? But: does it glorify God by being faithful to the specific work God has commissioned in this place, for these people, in this moment?

## PART IV — THREE CRITERIA AND THE INDIAN CONSEQUENCE
*(8 minutes)*

Sr Inigo proposes three criteria for evaluating every act of communication in the digital age. I want to engage each theologically, because together they constitute a practical application of the incarnational model.

**The first criterion: truthfulness — refuse the culture of false information.**

The theological grounding is *John 14:6*: *"I am the way, the truth, and the life."* Jesus does not say: I have truth, or I teach truth, or I promote truth. He says: I *am* truth. Truth, in the Fourth Gospel, is not a quality of propositions but a property of personal existence. And the community that follows this Jesus is, by definition, committed to a mode of communication that is truthful — in its content, its method, and its intent — because untruth is not merely a social problem. It is a Christological betrayal.

We are living in what is called the post-truth era — a cultural moment in which the emotional impact of a claim has become more socially powerful than its accuracy. WhatsApp forwards travel faster than fact-checks. Inflammatory claims reach millions before measured response catches its breath. And religious communities — who should be committed by their deepest theological convictions to truthfulness — are not immune. Every unverified forward shared. Every inflammatory religious claim amplified without scrutiny. Every communal rumor passed along because it confirmed what we already believed. These are not merely social failings. In the register of *John 14:6*, they are acts of unfaithfulness to the one who is Truth.

In the Indian context specifically, this has a dimension that must be named. The post-truth culture, in the contemporary Indian political environment, has become an instrument of communal mobilization — the weaponizing of false information about religious minority communities in order to generate fear, hatred, and violence. The religious person who shares, forwards, or amplifies such material — even unknowingly — is not neutral. They have become, however unwittingly, an instrument of that weaponization. Truthfulness, in the present Indian context, is not merely an epistemic virtue. It is a political and prophetic one.

**The second criterion: formation — give hope, strength, and consolation.**

Sr Inigo draws the model, as she consistently does, from Jesus himself — who did not merely transmit information to his disciples. He formed them. Three years of walking together, eating together, failing together, being questioned and challenged and sometimes corrected. The formation was not a curriculum. It was a relationship. And the criterion of good digital communication, by analogy, is whether it forms — whether it genuinely shapes the interior life of the person who encounters it in the direction of hope, of courage, of deeper truthfulness.

This is a demanding standard. Much religious digital content — even when well-intentioned — offers comfort rather than formation. It confirms what people already believe rather than challenging them to grow. It generates warm sentiment rather than genuine conversion of life. The formation criterion asks of every piece of content we produce: *"Does this actually make the person who encounters it more like the one it claims to represent?"*

**The third criterion: edification — build up through mercy.**

*Oikodomē* — the Greek word for edification — means, literally, the *building of a house*. The metaphor is architectural: communication that edifies is communication that constructs something — in the person, in the community, in the social fabric. And what it constructs, in the theological tradition, is shaped by mercy: the specific attention to the one who is broken, marginalized, overlooked, considered beyond repair.

In digital terms, edification means using whatever platform is available — however modest — to give visibility to those who have been rendered invisible by the dominant social order. The Dalit woman whose legal case needs a witness. The adivasi community whose land is being taken without adequate documentation of their protest. The undertrial prisoner in a West Bengal jail who has been waiting seven years and whose situation no one has publicly named. These are the specific, local, embodied applications of the edification criterion in this region.

And here is the historical precedent that Sr Inigo offers as evidence that this is possible: when the Vatican moved to excommunicate the Sri Lankan theologian Fr. Tissa Balasuriya on grounds that many serious theologians considered inadequate, the global Catholic community mobilized — through the then-nascent digital networks of the 1990s — to protest. The volume and quality of the response was sufficient to force a reconsideration. For the first time in Church history, the *sensus fidelium* — the faith sense of the whole people of God — found a digital vehicle adequate to its expression. The excommunication was rescinded.

This is not a precedent for digital activism as a substitute for theological discernment. It is a precedent for digital communication as a tool of the *sensus fidelium* — the prophetic voice of the whole community, finding expression through whatever channels are available, in the service of truth and justice.

The Indian consequence of all three criteria must be stated directly: in a social context of communal polarization, caste-inflected institutional life, and the specific marginalization of the communities that most of our charisms were founded to serve, the threefold standard of truthfulness, formation, and edification is not a generic communication ethics. It is a prophetic vocation. And the consecrated person who uses digital platforms in this context without theological intentionality — who posts, shares, and forwards without asking *"Is this true? Does this form? Does this edify?"* — has allowed the platform to become master rather than servant.

## PROPHETIC DEMAND & SILENCE
*(3 minutes)*

Let me close with a demand — and then with the silence that the demand requires.

The Incarnation is not merely a doctrine. It is the communication standard of God. God did not manage the world at arm's length. God pitched a tent in the midst of the community being addressed. God learned the language from the inside. God stopped in the middle of crowds for one person. God wept at a tomb. God called the dead by their name.

Until and unless the digital communication of consecrated persons is governed by this incarnational standard — until every platform decision is made by asking not *"How many people will this reach?"* but *"Does this serve the specific, embodied, relational communication that the God who became flesh modeled?"* — the digital apostolate will remain a form of ecclesiastical broadcasting rather than a participation in the mission of the incarnate *Logos*.

The question I leave with you is one question:

**In your present ministerial life, what is the ratio between your digital output and your incarnational presence — and does that ratio reflect a theology of communication adequate to the God who pitched a tent among us?**

*(Sustained silence — minimum four minutes. Do not fill it.)*

## CLOSING PRAYER

*God of the pitched tent,
God of the Word who became flesh
and chose, above every available distance,
the specific inconvenience of proximity —

We confess that we have, in many of our choices,
preferred the broadcast to the presence,
the platform to the person,
the notification to the knock on the door.

We have measured our pastoral vitality
in followers and views and reach
when You measured Yours
in twelve people around a table,
and one woman at a well,
and one dead man called by his name.

Forgive us the screens behind which we hide
when what was asked of us was simply a face —
present, unhurried, unguarded, genuinely moved.

Give us the courage to pitch our tent
in the places that are inconvenient and unremarkable
and produce no content worth posting —
the hospital room where someone is dying,
the jail corridor where someone has been forgotten,
the community kitchen where someone's name
no one on our staff knows.

Let every word we speak and post and share
be governed by three questions:
Is it true?
Does it form?
Does it build up the one who has been pulled down?

And let the only metric that finally matters be Yours:
Did it glorify You?

Amen.*

## SPEAKER NOTES

**Register:** Academic formation talk — the register of Fr. Joseph Lobo SJ. Structured theological argument, prophetically sober, with the homily skill's oral warmth entering most fully in the scriptural section and the closing prayer. The topic invites levity in its opening — resist the temptation to keep it light. The argument must carry its theological weight throughout.

**Key theological moves:**

1. The **broadcast model vs. incarnational model** of communication — with the specific pathologies of the broadcast model named structurally, not merely described culturally.
2. ***Logos*** recovered etymologically from *"content"* or *"message"* back to the Word who became flesh (*eskēnōsen* — pitched his tent) — with Marshall McLuhan's medium-message analysis as the secular complement identifying when the conversion occurred.
3. The **scriptural constellation**: John 1:14 (*eskēnōsen* as tent-pitching theology of presence), Luke 4:40 (the *"each of them"* of particularizing attention), John 11:21-35 (*embrimaomai* — the visceral affect of genuine presence), John 17:4 (the criterion of faithful commission rather than measurable impact).
4. The **three criteria** — truthfulness, formation, edification — engaged theologically with *John 14:6*, the formation model of Jesus, and the *oikodomē* metaphor, applied concretely to the Indian context of communal polarization and caste-inflected digital culture.
