## OPENING: THE PROBLEMATIC OF THE ECOLOGICAL CRISIS
*(7 minutes)*

By way of introducing this third reflection in our series, let me begin with an affirmation that every congregation in this room would subscribe to, and then with a reality that contradicts it in ways we have not yet adequately faced.

We say: creation is the gift of God, entrusted to human stewardship. The Second Vatican Council in *Gaudium et Spes* 69 affirms that God destined the earth and all it contains for all peoples and nations. Pope Francis in *Laudato Si'* 1 opens with the evocative address *"Laudato si', mi' Signore"* — "Praised be You, my Lord" — invoking Francis of Assisi's Canticle of the Creatures, and he argues throughout that care for creation is not a peripheral concern of Christian life but is constitutive of it. And *1 Timothy 4:4* — the anchor text of this reflection — says with absolute simplicity: *"Every creature of God is good."*

That is the claim.

And now the reality.

In the year 2020, a virus — invisible to the naked eye, carrying no institutional affiliation, recognizing no national boundary — brought the entire industrial civilization of the planet to a halt. Within weeks of the first lockdown, the rivers of northern India, clogged for decades with industrial and chemical waste, began to run clear. The Himalayan peaks became visible from the plains of Punjab for the first time in a generation. Species that had been retreating from human encroachment began, tentatively, to return. The sky above Delhi turned a color that children had previously seen only in photographs.

The earth healed — the moment we stepped back.

That fact is not merely an ecological observation. It is a theological indictment. And the indictment is this: the ecological crisis is not, at its root, a failure of technology or policy. It is a failure of theology. It is the consequence of a specific, historically locatable, theologically diagnosable distortion in how Christian civilization has understood the relationship between the human person, creation, and God.

The consequences of this distortion are no longer abstract. They are measured in parts per million of atmospheric carbon. They are visible in the retreat of the Gangotri glacier that feeds the Ganges. They are audible in the documented disappearance of bird species from the Sundarbans. They are present in the bodies of fishing communities on the coast of West Bengal whose livelihoods have been destroyed by the destruction of mangrove ecosystems.

And they are present — though we rarely name it this way — in our religious communities, in the gap between our theology of creation and the actual ecological footprint of our institutions.

In one sentence, if I were to state the thesis of this reflection: **The ecological crisis demands from consecrated persons not merely a change of lifestyle or a programme of environmental awareness, but what Pope Francis rightly calls an "ecological conversion" — a fundamental reorientation of the theological imagination that governs how we understand creation, our place within it, and our responsibility toward it.**

Let me develop this across four movements.

## PART I — TWO THEOLOGIES OF CREATION
*(10 minutes)*

There are, I would argue, two operative theologies of creation present within the Christian tradition — one dominant and one suppressed. They have produced, over centuries, radically different relationships to the natural world. And the ecological crisis is, in large part, the consequence of the dominance of the first theology over the second.

I shall call them, for the sake of precision, the *dominion theology* and the *covenant theology* of creation.

**The dominion theology** takes its primary textual warrant from *Genesis 1:28*: *"Be fruitful and multiply, and fill the earth and subdue it; and have dominion over the fish of the sea and over the birds of the air and over every living thing."* It reads *dominion* — the Hebrew *radah* — as a license for human mastery over the natural world. It understands creation as ordered instrumentally toward the human: the non-human exists for the use of the human, whose dignity and destiny are entirely distinct from, and superior to, the rest of created order.

This theology is not without textual support. But its reading of the text is selective. And the consequences of that selective reading, when married to the technological capacities of industrial capitalism, have been catastrophic.

The sociologist Max Weber, in his analysis of the relationship between Protestant Christianity and the development of capitalism, noted that the disenchantment of nature — the progressive stripping of the sacred from the natural world — was a condition of possibility for industrial exploitation of the environment. When the sacred is evacuated from the forest, the forest becomes timber. When the sacred is removed from the river, the river becomes a resource. Weber intended this as a sociological observation, not a theological judgment. But the theological judgment follows directly: a Christianity that desacralizes creation opens it to exploitation.

**The covenant theology** of creation reads the same Genesis texts differently and more completely. It attends, first, to the *Sabbath*. In *Genesis 2:1-3*, God rests — and the Sabbath rest is extended in the Mosaic legislation (*Leviticus 25:1-7*) to the land itself. The land has a Sabbath. It must lie fallow. It has its own claim on rest, independent of its usefulness to human agriculture. The land is not merely an object of human action; it is a subject with its own rhythm, its own integrity, its own place within God's order.

The covenant theology attends, second, to the text that is almost never preached: *Genesis 9:9-10*. God, speaking after the Flood, says: *"I now establish my covenant with you and with your descendants after you — and with every living creature that is with you: the birds, the domestic animals, and every animal of the earth with you."* This is the first covenant in the biblical narrative. And it is explicitly tripartite: between God, the human person, and *every living creature*. The non-human creation is not merely a backdrop to the covenant. It is a party to it.

Pope Francis, in *Laudato Si'* 66-67, draws precisely this distinction when he argues that we must move from viewing the world as *nature* — a system available for human use — to viewing it as *creation* — a sacred gift, bearing the mark of God's love, entrusted to human care rather than human exploitation. The difference between *nature* and *creation* is not scientific. It is theological. And until the theological imagination is converted, no programme of environmental action will address the root of the problem.

This is what Pope Saint John Paul II meant when, in his 1990 World Day of Peace message, he spoke of what I would call *an ecological original sin*: a fundamental disorder in the human relationship to creation that has its roots in a disordered self-understanding — the illusion of human sovereignty over creation rather than human *co-participation* within it.

The two models produce, in practice, two very different religious communities.

A community operating within the dominion theology — however unself-consciously — treats its property as resource: the land on which the convent stands, the water it consumes, the energy it uses, the food it wastes. It applies to the natural world the same logic of acquisition and consumption that it applies to its institutional development. And it experiences no theological dissonance in doing so, because its operative theology has told it that the natural world exists for human use.

A community operating within the covenant theology understands its relationship to the land it occupies, the water it draws, the energy it consumes, as a theological relationship — a participation in the first covenant God made with all living creatures. Its stewardship of creation is not an addition to its religious life. It is an expression of it.

## PART II — ETYMOLOGICAL RECOVERY: *KTISIS* AND THE FIRST COVENANT
*(9 minutes)*

Let me now perform the etymological recovery that this argument requires.

The Greek word for creation in the New Testament is *ktisis* — from the verb *ktizō*, meaning to found, to establish, to bring into being from one's own initiative. It is used by Paul in *Romans 8:19-22* in the passage I shall come to in a moment — and it carries a meaning that is almost entirely lost in the English rendering "creation."

*Ktisis* is not a product. It is not raw material. It is not a resource pool. *Ktisis* is what comes into being through an act of founding love — and the one who founds it remains in relationship with what has been founded. The *ktisis* belongs to the *ktizō* — to the one who calls it into being. And the human person, within the *ktisis*, is not the owner but the custodian.

Now, what has happened to *ktisis* in the dominant tradition of Western Christianity is precisely what happened to *consecrare* and *charisma* in the distortions we examined in our first two talks: it has been converted from a relational term into an object term. *Creation* — from a living network of creaturely relationships, each bearing the mark of its divine origin — became *nature*: a system of objects available for human use and classification.

The historical moment of this conversion can be located with some precision. It is, as the historian Lynn White Jr. argued in his influential 1967 essay on the historical roots of the ecological crisis, substantially a consequence of the synthesis between medieval Christian theology and the emerging empirical sciences — a synthesis that progressively disenchanted the natural world by relocating the sacred entirely within the human and the divine, and evacuating it from the non-human. The tree becomes wood. The river becomes water. The animal becomes protein.

Let me be careful here. White's thesis is contested, and the tradition contains, alongside the dominion stream, a powerful sacramental stream that has always seen creation as *vestiges Dei* — traces of God. Bonaventure's theology of creation as a book written by God is one expression of this stream. Francis of Assisi's Canticle of the Creatures is another. The Celtic monastic tradition's profound attentiveness to the natural world is another.

But the sacramental stream has been — in the post-Tridentine, post-industrial period — systematically marginalized within the dominant theological formation of clergy and religious. We are formed to read Scripture, to study dogmatic theology, to learn the structures of liturgy and canon law. We are not formed to read the book of creation alongside the book of Scripture. And the consequence is that the ecological literacy of the average religious is vastly inferior to their theological literacy — which is itself a theological problem, because Augustine, Basil the Great, and Maximus the Confessor all understood creation as a second book of divine revelation alongside Scripture.

The recovery of *ktisis* as a relational, not merely objective, term — the recovery of creation as God's founding gift that remains in relationship with its Founder — is what *Laudato Si'* attempts when it insists on the language of *"our common home."* Home is a relational word. You do not exploit your home. You do not extract from it indifferently. You inhabit it, care for it, and are shaped by it.

## PART III — THE SCRIPTURAL CONSTELLATION
*(9 minutes)*

Let me bring four scriptural texts into this argument — each adding a dimension that the others do not supply.

**The first witness: Genesis 9:9-10.**

*"I now establish my covenant with you and with your descendants after you — and with every living creature that is with you."*

I have already drawn attention to the tripartite structure of this covenant: God, the human community, and the whole community of living creatures. But I want to press further into the theological implication of this structure.

A covenant, in the biblical tradition, is not a contract. It is a bond of belonging — a relationship that creates mutual obligation and carries consequence for its violation. The prophetic tradition — Isaiah, Hosea, Jeremiah — is explicit that Israel's violation of its covenant with God produces consequences in the natural world. Isaiah 24:4-6 states it with remarkable directness: *"The earth mourns and withers... The earth is polluted by its inhabitants, for they have transgressed the laws, violated the statutes, broken the everlasting covenant."* The word *polluted* here — *haneph* in Hebrew — means profaned, desecrated. And the everlasting covenant that has been broken is precisely the covenant of Genesis 9: the covenant with every living creature.

The ecological crisis, read through this prophetic text, is not merely an environmental emergency. It is a covenantal crisis — a sign that the first covenant has been broken on a planetary scale.

**The second witness: Romans 8:19-22.**

*"For the creation waits with eager longing for the revealing of the children of God... We know that the whole creation has been groaning in labor pains until now."*

Paul's use of *ktisis* here is extraordinary. He does not speak of creation as passive, as background, as resource. He speaks of it as *waiting* — with *eager longing*, the Greek *apokaradokia*, which means literally a craning of the neck, a straining forward in anticipation. Creation is not indifferent to its own condition. It is expectant.

And Paul connects the redemption of creation to the *"revealing of the children of God."* This is a claim with direct consequences for our understanding of consecrated life. The redemption of creation is not something that happens to creation independently of the human community. It happens *through* the revealing of those who have received, in the language of John 1:12, the right to be children of God. The ecological responsibility of the consecrated person is not incidental to their vocation. It is a constitutive dimension of it.

**The third witness: Matthew 6:26-28.**

*"Look at the birds of the air... Consider the lilies of the field."*

The teaching method of Jesus is itself an ecological argument. He does not direct his disciples to the library. He directs them to the field. He does not illustrate the love of the Father with an administrative metaphor or a juridical category. He illustrates it with a bird — a creature of no economic utility, which neither produces surplus nor accumulates capital — and with a flower, which exists for a season and is gone. And he says: look at these. Learn from these. Let these teach you something about the Father's care that no theological text will tell you.

Walter Brueggemann rightly observes that the prophetic imagination requires alternative forms of consciousness — ways of perceiving reality that are not available within the dominant social and economic logic. The natural world — stripped of its instrumental value, attended to in its own integrity — is precisely such an alternative form of consciousness. Jesus was not offering his disciples a nature lesson. He was offering them a theological curriculum that the dominant religious world of his day had ceased to provide.

**The fourth witness: Revelation 22:1-2.**

*"The river of the water of life... flows from the throne of God and of the Lamb through the middle of the street of the city. On either side of the river is the tree of life with its twelve kinds of fruit... and the leaves of the tree are for the healing of the nations."*

The final vision of the redeemed cosmos in the Christian canon is not an abstract spiritual state. It is a garden. It is a river. It is a tree. The *eschaton* — the ultimate goal of God's redemptive purpose — is not the dissolution of creation but its restoration and transfiguration. The tree that was present at the beginning of the human story (*Genesis 2:9*) is present at its end — now yielding not merely fruit but *healing for the nations*.

This eschatological reading has direct ecological consequences. If the goal of God's redemption includes the restoration of creation — if the *ktisis* that groans (*Romans 8:22*) is the same *ktisis* that will be set free — then the destruction of creation is not merely a social or environmental problem. It is an eschatological problem. It is the anticipatory destruction of what God intends to redeem.

## PART IV — THE HISTORICAL DIAGNOSIS AND ITS INDIAN CONSEQUENCE
*(7 minutes)*

The historical diagnosis I want to offer here is this: the ecological crisis in its present form is the convergence of two distortions — one theological, one economic — that have been centuries in the making.

The theological distortion I have already named: the dominion reading of Genesis, the progressive desacralization of nature, the evacuation of the sacred from the non-human world. This distortion did not originate with the industrial revolution. But the industrial revolution gave it technological instruments of implementation at a scale previously unavailable.

The economic distortion is the logic of what Karl Polanyi called the *"self-regulating market"* — a system in which all things, including land, water, and living creatures, are treated as commodities: objects of exchange, available to the highest bidder, valued only by their market price. Polanyi argued in *The Great Transformation* (1944) that this commodification of nature — what he called the *fictitious commodity* of land — was one of the central pathologies of modern capitalism, and that it would produce social and ecological consequences that the market itself could neither prevent nor repair.

The convergence of these two distortions — the theological desacralization and the economic commodification of nature — is what has produced the crisis *Laudato Si'* describes. And Pope Francis is absolutely correct that a merely technological or policy response is insufficient. What is required is what he calls, in *Laudato Si'* 216-221, an *"ecological conversion"* — a transformation of the interior life that then transforms the external relationship to creation.

Now, the Indian consequence of this diagnosis must be named directly, and it is threefold.

First: the communities most severely affected by ecological destruction in this region are the communities that religious congregations were founded to serve. The fishing communities of the Sundarbans — whose livelihoods depend on the health of the mangrove ecosystem, now threatened by both sea-level rise and commercial aquaculture — are among the poorest and most marginalized populations in West Bengal. The adivasi communities of Jharkhand and the forested regions of this state, whose entire spiritual world — their cosmology, their ritual life, their social structure — is constituted by their relationship to the forest, are the communities most directly displaced by mining, deforestation, and extractive industry. The ecological crisis is not an abstract global problem. It is a concrete local injustice against the people our charisms sent us to serve.

Second: the congregations present in this room are, in most cases, significant institutional actors in this region. They own land. They manage large buildings. They consume energy, water, and food at institutional scale. The ecological footprint of a single convent or seminary is not negligible. And the question that follows from the covenant theology of creation is not: are we aware of the ecological crisis? It is: what does the ecological footprint of our institution communicate about our theology of creation? What does our waste say about our vow of poverty? What does our energy consumption say about our relationship to the first covenant God made with every living creature?

Third: there is, in the adivasi and dalit communities of this region, an ecological wisdom that the dominant theological tradition has consistently marginalized and sometimes actively suppressed. The relational understanding of the human person's place within the natural community — an understanding embedded in the cosmologies of the Santali, the Mundari, and the other indigenous communities of this region — is not primitive animism to be replaced by Christian theology. It is, in many respects, a more adequate expression of the covenant theology of creation than the dominion theology that missionary Christianity often brought with it.

George Soares-Prabhu SJ, in his analysis of Indian contextual theology, argued that the triple alienation of Indian Christian theology — from the Indian intellectual tradition, from the Indian poor, and from the Indian soil — was itself a theological pathology. Ecological conversion, in the Indian context, requires a recovery of solidarity with the soil — a willingness to be taught by the communities who have never forgotten what the dominion theology caused so many to unlearn.

## PROPHETIC DEMAND & SILENCE
*(3 minutes)*

Let me close with a demand.

Until and unless ecological conversion is understood as a theological category — not a lifestyle choice, not a programme of environmental awareness, but a fundamental reorientation of how we understand creation, covenant, and our place within both — the religious communities of this region will continue to speak with prophetic urgency about justice for the poor while presiding, with institutional indifference, over the destruction of the earth on which the poor depend for their lives.

The Brazilian Bishops' Conference, in their ecological examination of conscience, named seven ecological sins. Each of them is, in the theological analysis I have been developing, an expression of the dominion theology applied to concrete life: consumption without reverence, waste without guilt, apathy without conversion. And Pope Francis has proposed — with deliberate and significant intent — a new eighth work of mercy: *care for our common home*. Planting a tree is an act of mercy. Reducing institutional waste is an act of mercy. Choosing the river over the drain is an act of mercy.

The question I leave you with is this — one question, not a programme:

**In your congregation's present institutional life, what is the gap between your theology of creation and your actual relationship to the created world you inhabit — and what specific, structural, non-cosmetic step are you prepared to take to narrow that gap?**

*(Sustained silence — minimum four minutes.)*

## CLOSING PRAYER

*God of the first covenant —
of the birds and the fish and the beasts of the field,
of the river that ran clear when we stepped back,
of the Himalayan sky that turned blue again
when the factories were briefly silent —

We confess that we have been parties to the breaking of the covenant You made
not only with us, but with every living creature.

We have read Your word in Scripture
and failed to read it in the second book —
the book of creation
that Augustine and Basil and Francis
knew how to open,
and that we have learned to walk past
on the way to the library.

Forgive us not quickly.
Let the confession do its work.

And then — when we are ready —
send us back to the earth
as its custodians, not its conquerors.
As children of the covenant,
not masters of the resource.

In the Sundarbans, in the Teesta valley,
in the forested hills of Jharkhand,
in the slum compounds of Kolkata
where the soil has forgotten it was once alive —
let our presence be
the beginning of a covenantal healing.

Let us be the ones who plant the tree
whose shade we will never sit under.
Let us be the ones who clean the river
we did not dirty alone.
Let us be the ones who learn, at last,
from the communities whose entire prayer
has always been the forest.

We ask this in the name of the one
in whose act of creation every creature was called good —
and in whose act of redemption
even the groaning *ktisis*
is promised a share in the liberty
of the children of God.

Amen.*

## SPEAKER NOTES

**Register:** Academic formation talk — the register of Fr. Joseph Lobo SJ. Structured theological argument, prophetically sober tone, with the homily skill's oral warmth entering most fully in the scriptural section and the closing prayer. The ecological content must not become environmental advocacy in a secular register — it must remain, throughout, a theological argument with pastoral and structural consequences.

**Key theological moves:**
1. The **dominion theology vs. covenant theology** of creation — a conceptual pair with historically traceable and structurally visible consequences.
2. ***Ktisis*** recovered etymologically from object-noun to relational term — from raw material to God's founding gift that remains in relationship with its Founder.
3. The **scriptural constellation**: Genesis 9 (tripartite covenant), Romans 8 (*apokaradokia* — creation's eager longing), Matthew 6 (Jesus's ecological pedagogy), Revelation 22 (eschatological restoration of creation).
4. The **historical diagnosis**: the convergence of theological desacralization (dominion reading of Genesis, Lynn White Jr.'s thesis) and economic commodification (Polanyi's fictitious commodity of land).
5. The **Indian consequence** stated in three registers: the communities most affected (Sundarbans fishing communities, adivasi forest communities), the institutional ecological footprint of religious congregations, and the ecological wisdom of indigenous communities as a theological resource rather than a missionary problem.

**Secular thinkers deployed:**
- Max Weber: disenchantment of nature as a condition of industrial exploitation (sociological complement)
- Karl Polanyi: commodification of land as fictitious commodity (economic complement)
